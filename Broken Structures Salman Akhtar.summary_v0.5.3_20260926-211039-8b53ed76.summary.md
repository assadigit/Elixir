### 1. 6 - PARANOID PERSONALITY DISORDER

This chapter presents a unified phenomenological framework for Paranoid Personality Disorder (PPD), synthesizing fragmented descriptive and psychoanalytic literature into a cohesive clinical profile. It argues that PPD, historically under-studied despite its inclusion in the DSM, is defined not by delusions but by a paradoxical combination of traits: profound mistrust coexisting with naive gullibility, arrogant demandingness paired with hidden inferiority, and emotional coldness alongside marked sensitivity. The descriptive history traces the condition from Kraepelin’s “paranoid personality” to modern conceptualizations, highlighting these core contradictions as essential to understanding the disorder.

The psychoanalytic section explores the developmental underpinnings of PPD, moving beyond Freud’s libidinal focus to emphasize hostility rooted in sadomasochism, preoedipal aggression, and severe infantile trauma. Key contributors such as Klein, Winnicott, and Erikson identify failures in basic trust, separation-individuation, and the development of ambivalence as primary drivers. Blum’s concept of the “inconstant object” further links PPD to an inability to integrate love and fear of attachment.

Critiquing the DSM-III-R for omitting crucial features like self-esteem defects and naive gullibility, the author proposes a composite profile organized into six areas of psychosocial functioning: self-concept, interpersonal relations, social adaptation, love/sexuality, ethics, and cognitive style. This model distinguishes between overt manifestations (e.g., hypervigilance) and covert features (e.g., timidity), arguing that this integration of phenomenology and psychostructure is superior to simple checklists for differential diagnosis. The chapter concludes by differentiating PPD from psychotic paranoias, phobic character, and narcissistic or antisocial disorders, emphasizing the need to recognize the “mirror complementarity” of the divided self to prevent misdiagnosis and understand the complex interplay of constitutional, drive-related, and developmental factors.
