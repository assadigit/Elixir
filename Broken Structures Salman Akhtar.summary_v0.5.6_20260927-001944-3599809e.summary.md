### 1. 6 - PARANOID PERSONALITY DISORDER

This chapter provides a full survey of paranoid personality disorder, combining descriptive psychiatric and psychoanalytic literature to build a complete clinical profile. The author reviews early historical definitions from Magnan to Kraepelin, then moves through mid-century phenomenological and psychoanalytic views. Key contributors include Bleuler, Jaspers, Schneider, Leonhard, Shepherd, and Polatin in the descriptive track, and Freud, Klein, Winnicott, Erikson, Heimann, Rycroft, Cameron, Shapiro, Jacobson, Bursten, Kernberg, Meissner, and Blum in the psychoanalytic track. The DSM-III and DSM-III-R criteria are critiqued for missing core contradictions such as gullibility alongside mistrust and grandiosity. A composite profile of six psychosocial domains is proposed, each with overt and covert features. Differential diagnosis covers paranoid schizophrenia, delusional disorder, phobic character, compulsive personality, narcissistic personality, and antisocial personality. The chapter ends by noting unresolved questions about subtypes and lifespan variation.

**Key takeaways:**
- Paranoid personality disorder is a well-established nosological entity listed in DSM-III-R and all prior DSM versions, yet specific papers on its phenomenology are scarce (p. 137).
- Magnan (1893) divided paranoid psychoses into chronic delusional state of systematic evolution and delusional states of degenerates, with the latter split into three subtypes: paranoia associated with mental defect, chronic delusional states with good long-term prognosis, and bouffee delirante des degeneres characterized by sudden onset, affective coloring, clouded sensorium, and rapid remission (p. 138).
- Magnan described the "fragile personality" behind short-term paranoid developments as showing idiosyncratic thinking, undue sensitivity, hypochondriasis, referential thinking, and suspiciousness (p. 138).
- Kraepelin (1905) described a pseudoquerulous personality type always on alert to find a grievance but without delusions; he renamed it paranoid personality in 1921 with a more detailed description of marked distrust, feeling unjustly treated, and vulnerability to erotomanialike secret love affairs (p. 138).
- Kraepelin noted a contradiction: such individuals think their unusual actions are quite in order yet are often extremely credulous and accept gossip as truth (p. 138).
- Bleuler (1908) described "contentious psychopathy" or "paranoid constitution" with a triad of suspiciousness, grandiosity, and feelings of persecution, emphasizing that false assumptions do not attain the form of real delusion (p. 139).
- Kretschmer (1927) emphasized the sensitive inner core of the paranoia-prone personality, noting contradictory attitudes of feeling shy and inadequate while maintaining an attitude of entitlement, often pertaining to sexual prowess (p. 139).
- Jaspers (1949) described "self-insecure personalities" whose life is brought about by outside experiences and their interpretation; they seek external confirmation of self-depreciation, see intentional insults in others' behavior, and overcompensate with compulsive formality and exaggerated displays of assurance (p. 139).
- Schneider (1950) described "fanatic psychopaths" in two types: combative (unpleasantly insistent, actively quarrelsome, litigious) and eccentric (passive, secretive, vulnerable to esoteric sects, harboring suspicious assumptions) (p. 139).
- Leonhard (1959) emphasized that paranoid individuals overvalue their abilities, attribute failure to the ill will of others, and are ambitious, driven, and inordinately sensitive to criticism (p. 140).
- Shepherd (1961) provided a thorough profile of morbid jealousy in paranoid personality: constant feeling of mistreatment, accumulation of trivial incidents, effort to furnish proof of accusations, and extreme interpersonal disturbance with spouses, friends, or legal authorities (p. 140).
- Polatin (1975) described paranoid individuals as rigid, suspicious, watchful, self-centered, selfish, inwardly highly sensitive yet emotionally undemonstrative, arrogant, secretive, critical, humorless, inconsiderate, with a facade of affability that shatters to reveal mistrust, authoritarianism, hate, and rage (p. 140).
- Subsequent textbook descriptions remained relatively uniform; Hamilton (1974, 1984) distinguished sensitive paranoid personality (subdued resentment and cynicism) from querulous paranoid personality (open defiance and litigiousness) (p. 141).
- Slater and Roth (1977) noted the "contradictory pride and lack of self-confidence"; Salzman (1974) noted coexistence of superiority and inferiority; Stanton (1978) noted overt stubbornness with hidden self-doubt and contempt for others' naivete alongside latent gullibility (p. 141).
- In the 1980s, attention shifted to paranoid psychoses rather than paranoid personality disorder; reviews by Morey (1988) and Widiger and Frances (1985) paid scant attention, attributing omission to lack of controversy caused by understudy rather than understanding (p. 141).
- A computer search of personality disorder literature from 1985 to 1988 yielded over 1,200 citations, none specifically devoted to the phenomenology of paranoid personality disorder (Gorton and Akhtar 1990) (p. 141).
- Millon (1981) divided features of paranoid personality into four categories: behavioral characteristics of vigilance, abrasive irritability, and counterattack; complaints indicating oversensitivity, social isolation, and mistrust; dynamics of denying personal insecurities, attributing these to others, and selfinflation through grandiose fantasies; and a coping style of detesting dependence and hostile distancing (p. 141).
- Tobak (1989) observed the frequent coexistence of expedient mendacity with moralistic selfrighteousness in the paranoid character (p. 141).
- Freud (1911) related paranoid tendencies to repudiation of latent homosexuality through projection, transforming homosexual longings into hatred and projecting it externally so the love object appears persecutory (p. 142).
- This formulation was criticized by Meissner (1978b), Ovessey (1955), and Walters (1955) for overemphasizing libidinal factors, ignoring actual harshness faced while growing up, and minimizing aggression's role (p. 142).
- Blum (1980) highlighted that Freud (1887-1902, 1908b) hinted at the deep connection between sadomasochism and paranoid psychopathology on two earlier occasions (p. 142).
- Freud (1919) stated that people with unconscious beating fantasies develop special sensitiveness toward father-like figures and may bring about the imagined situation of being beaten by the father, which could be the basis of delusional litigiousness of paranoia (p. 142).
- In later writings, Freud called attention to three factors in paranoid pathology: initial aggressive disposition (1923a), actual early experiences of threat to survival (1922), and marked aggression toward the mother during preoedipal phase (1923) (p. 142).
- Klein (1946) described the "paranoid-schizoid position" as the earliest developmental constellation of infantile object relations, characterized by extensive projection creating persecutory anxiety defended by splitting, denial, and primitive idealization (p. 143).
- Winnicott (1952) emphasized external environment's role in paranoid traits: infant experiences interference with "going-on-being" as menace; maternal failures lead to disruption, anxiety, inner withdrawal, and diminution of psychic freedom, later producing suspiciousness, lack of playfulness, motor stiffness, preoccupation with fantasies of cruelty, social isolation, persecutory dreams, sadomasochistic sexual concerns, and paranoid fantasies (p. 143).
- Erikson (1950a) posited that paranoid trends arise from overwhelming early infantile frustrations impeding basic trust development, leading to lack of confidence, helplessness, pessimism, shame and doubt, rigid superego morality, inhibition of spontaneity, deterioration of peer relationships, omnipotent fantasies masking inferiority, and identity diffusion with negativism, self-isolation, and avoidance of intimacy (p. 143).
- Heimann (1952) likened paranoid personality to chronic hypomanic state; both are defenses against deep unworthiness, rage, guilt, and depression; similarities include triumphant feeling convicting objects of incompetence, impaired cognitive processes such as shallowness and inability to accept differing viewpoints, and sufficient ego functions including sharp observation, hairsplitting, rich vocabulary, and gifted speech (p. 143).
- Heimann noted that paranoid defensive operations are primitive, centering on splitting, denial, and projective identification; they need "bad" external objects for projection, yet remain infiltrated with disavowed drive derivatives giving sadistic pleasure in persecutory situations (p. 143).
- Rycroft (1960) reported treatment of an individual with paranoid personality showing profound mistrust, pervasive contradictions, ill-synthesized identity, idiosyncratic speech, pretensions to genius compensating for lack of object love, denial of dependent longings, and guarding against loving; origin traced to severe infantile traumata such as early parental loss with shocking betrayal of anaclitic needs (p. 143).
- Cameron (1963) stated many paranoid individuals were treated cruelly in childhood, internalizing sadistic attitudes; outlined four aspects: hypersensitivity to unconscious attitudes of others; insensitivity to one's own motivations; secret feeling of sexual inferiority; lack of self-esteem and powerful undercurrent of unconscious guilt (p. 144).
- Cameron noted paranoid approaches sexuality expecting rebuffal, selective hypersensitivity to unconscious homoeroticism and pregenital trends in others, vulnerability to erotomania from seeing full-blown erotic intention in minimal libidinal cues (p. 144).
- Shapiro (1965) recognized two types: furtive constricted apprehensively suspicious individuals and rigidly arrogant aggressively suspicious megalomanic ones; described paranoid cognition as rigidity of mental set, intentional disregard of new data, intolerance of ambiguity, search for clues confirming bias, tensely directed hyperactive scanning attention, and partial loss of reality (p. 144).
- Shapiro described the paranoid person's subjective world as a blend of autistic and factual: biased seizing of "significant" clues combined with loss of appreciation of context that gives clues significance; interpretively autistic but accurate in factual details (p. 145).
- Shapiro noted inhibition of spontaneity, caricatured superficial affability, motor tension, hyperintentionality where every action has an aim and is designed, abandonment of tenderness sensuality and playfulness, lack of interest in art music poetry with fascination for mechanical devices computers electrical circuits automation schemes (p. 145).
- Jacobson (1971) observed paranoid individuals frequently grew up in families with overt cruelty and bickering between parents; sadomasochistic family atmosphere often focusing on a parent's marital infidelity sets stage for child's own sadism; severe early frustrations prevent unambivalent object relations and stable identifications, weaken self-esteem, create sense of futility about finding love (p. 145).
- Bursten (1973a) suggested paranoid personality is a subtype of narcissistic personality; fundamental dynamic issue is powerful unconscious longing for reunion with infantile parental objects stirring homosexual incestuous fears and dread of dissolution of self; these shameful desires are disavowed by projection and negated through ruthless contempt (p. 145).
- Kernberg (1975a) associated paranoid personality with borderline personality organization or lower level character pathology; superego integration minimal, synthetic ego function impaired; splitting denial projective identification replace repression; restriction of conflict-free ego; primary-process thinking infiltrates cognition (p. 146).
- Meissner (1978b) noted frequent presentation through depressed masochistic wife unable to tolerate home situation with paranoid husband resistant to treatment; believes paranoid process highly structuralized and ego-syntonically embedded; core consists of displacement of responsibility through projection, suspiciousness, defensive grandiosity, rigid conviction about beliefs, concern over autonomy, troubled relationships with authority figures (p. 146).
- Meissner agrees with classic view of paranoia as defense against latent homosexuality but posits underlying identification with weak passive victimized mother is central source of anxiety in both conditions (p. 146).
- Blum (1980, 1981) highlighted less-cited Freud views and synthesized classic and contemporary psychoanalytic writings; states hostility is primary problem not merely defense against latent homosexual impulses; does not exclude reverse formulation but regards it as secondary (p. 147).
- Blum posits complex interplay of innate disposition, actual threats to survival in childhood, impaired object relations, structural defects, and defensive elaborations underlie adult paranoid picture; "latent core" involves preoedipal problems (p. 147).
- Blum, interweaving Mahler's (1975) separation-individuation theory, points out lack of internalization of comforting constant mother associates with lack of ego integration, untamed infantile omnipotence, fragile self-esteem, intense separation anxiety; introduces "inconstant object" — ambivalently loved object felt both persecutory and needed; threat of betrayal must be tenaciously maintained (p. 147).
- Blum states constant fear of persecution is reciprocal of libidinal object constancy and desperate effort to preserve illusory constant object while fearing betrayal and loss; structural substrate of untamed narcissism, intense aggression, archaic defenses makes mastery of oedipal tasks difficult; masochistic negative perverse solutions predominate (p. 147).
- Other contributions bearing on phenomenology include Anthony's (1981) paranoid adolescent, Green's (1986) moral narcissism, Kohut's (1972) narcissistic rage, Pao's (1969) morbid jealousy, Socarides's (1966) affect of vengeance, Volkan's (1986) need for enemies and allies, Akhtar and Byrne's (1983) behavioral correlates of splitting, Akhtar's (1984) identity diffusion (p. 147).
- Paranoid rage negativism hatred are viewed as complexly determined phenomena: affective concomitants of disturbed ego, measures to control external objects compensating for lack of inner object constancy, defending against passive surrender to love objects that stirs dread of incest homosexuality dissolution of self (p. 148).
- DSM-III criteria included hypersensitivity indicated by at least two items: tendency to be easily slighted and quick to take offense, exaggeration of difficulties, readiness to counterattack when any threat is perceived, inability to relax; restricted affectivity indicated by at least two items: appearance of being cold and unemotional, pride taken in always being objective rational unemotional, lack of true sense of humor, absence of passive soft tender sentimental feelings (p. 149).
- DSM-III-R reduction from sixteen items to seven makes list more manageable and consistent with other personality disorder criteria; inclusion of "bears grudges" is improvement over DSM-III (p. 150).
- Both DSM-III and DSM-III-R do not include superego unevenness, self-esteem defects, romantic and sexual difficulties, core of naive gullibility, or coexistence of mutually contradictory stances in almost all areas of psychosocial function (p. 150).
- The composite profile (Table 6-1) organizes clinical features into six areas: self-concept, interpersonal relations, social adaptation, love and sexuality, ethics standards and ideals, cognitive style; each area has overt and covert manifestations (p. 151).
- Overt manifestations include arrogant mistrustful suspicious of others, driven industrious successful in solitary professions, unromantic idiosyncratically moralistic sharply vigilant toward external environment (p. 151).
- Covert manifestations include frightened timid gullible, chronically experiencing interpersonal difficulties in work, corruptible, vulnerable to erotomania and sadomasochistic perversions, cognitively unable to grasp totality of actual events in proper context (p. 151).
- The overt-covert designations do not necessarily imply conscious or unconscious existence; they denote seemingly contradictory phenomenological aspects more or less easily discernible; contradictions permeate self-concept, social adaptation, love life, morality, cognitive attitudes (p. 151).
- This conceptualization underscores centrality of splitting and projective mechanisms in paranoid makeup; gives sounder developmental and dynamic underpinnings; prepares clinicians for "mirror complementarity of the self" that Bach (1977) noted (p. 151).
- Risk exists that overt and covert designations get equated with conscious and unconscious topography despite reminder to contrary; risk heightened because covert features are difficult to discern; similar profiles of narcissistic, schizoid, hypomanic personalities faced questions about utilizability by dynamically unsophisticated psychiatrists (Frances et al. 1988); lack of actuarial data makes relative diagnostic weight difficult to ascertain (p. 151).
- The profile remains superior to ordinary checklist methods because it includes both phenomenological and psychoanalytic observations, values depth and complexity over descriptive oversimplification, establishes connection between behavioral and psychostructural hence developmental aspects of paranoid character pathology (p. 154).
- The profile may lend itself better to prototypal model of classification proposed by Frances and Widiger (1986) as only way to salvage categorical system of personality diagnosis; helps in meaningful differential diagnosis of paranoid from related personality disorders (p. 154).
- Differential diagnosis distinguishes paranoid personality disorder from paranoid schizophrenia: paranoid schizophrenic displays serious break with reality, bizarre delusions of persecution grandeur jealousy infidelity, auditory hallucinations, symptoms of thought control, incongruous affect, gross peculiarities of behavior; none characteristic of paranoid personality (p. 156).
- Reactive or acute paranoid disorders should be differentiated from paranoid personality disorder though they may coexist; acute paranoid disorder seen among individuals who experienced drastic environmental changes such as immigrants, refugees, prisoners of war, inductees into military services, people leaving home for first time; delusions often comprehensible resolve quickly rarely become chronic (p. 156).
- Difference between paranoia and paranoid personality seems largely quantitative per Bleuler (1908), Kraepelin (1905, 1921), Leonhard (1959), Stanton (1978); some authors such as Munro (1987) consider them separate very different disorders; paranoia has delusions while paranoid personality never does; kernel of truth is greater in paranoid personality suspicions; accusations have more plausibility; attitudes are diffuse and less encapsulated than in paranoia (p. 156).
- Phobic character was coined by Fenichel (1946) to designate individuals whose reactive behavior limits itself to avoidance of situations originally desired for; Mackinnon and Michels (1971) recognized fearful avoidance as characterological defense; Stone (1980) described "phobic-anxious" personality displaying extremes of fearfulness and avoidance of harmless objects and situations (p. 156).
- Phobic character differs from paranoid personality: they avoid situations not people; phobic feeling is "It is a danger but I do not know why" while paranoid feeling is "It is a danger and I know why namely such and such is his or her intention"; phobic lacks personalization and intentionality of outside menace (p. 157).
- Phobic personality should be regarded as higher level (Bak 1946) or neurotic character organization and "ego-syntonic characterological counterpart" (Akhtar 1986) of phobic neurosis proper; paranoid personality disorder shows lower level (Kernberg 1970) or borderline personality organization with pervasive splitting denial projective identification in setting of identity diffusion (p. 157).
- Paranoid and compulsive personalities are alike in rigidity, intense purposefulness, limited affective spontaneity, restricted sexuality, generalized difficulty with risk taking humor playfulness (Shapiro 1965); both dislike surprises prefer order predictability (p. 158).
- Compulsive knows pressure to conform emanates from within and regards moral dictates as unquestionably superior to instinctual desires; paranoid views such demands as originating externally and unjustified; compulsive's rebellion is subtle unconscious disguised ego-alien (Fenichel 1945); paranoid consciously plots against imagined enemies (p. 158).
- Developmental background differs: compulsive had successful separation-individuation process and acquired self-and object constancy; problems emanate largely from oedipal phase difficulties regressive substituted by anal phase metaphors; paranoid had much actual preoedipal trauma and unsuccessful separation-individuation, lacks self-and object constancy; superficial developmental history reveals more traumatized turbulent childhood in paranoid (p. 158).
- Many similarities between paranoid and narcissistic personality disorders led Kernberg (1985) to declare distinction particularly difficult; both are ambitious haughty driven given to narcissistic rage when pride injured (Kohut 1972); both display predominance of splitting over repression though projective mechanisms more marked in paranoid; both show arrested separation-individuation, distorted Oedipus complex, less than optimal latency adolescent passage (p. 158).
- Subtle identity diffusion often masked by overzealous yet shallow vocational commitment is discernible in both; superego unduly harsh uneven poorly demarcated from ego proper; cases with combined narcissistic and paranoid features may be more common than pure types (Kernberg 1985, Millon 1981) (p. 158).
- When overlap not marked, differences exist: narcissistic does not display pervasive mistrust litigiousness self-righteous indignation of paranoid; paranoid lacks charming social veneer seductiveness sexual promiscuity inordinate exploitativeness of narcissist; narcissist hides discomfort with spontaneity by crafty socialization while paranoid hides by angry negativism haughty aloofness brooding withdrawal (p. 159).
- Cognitively, narcissist displays absentmindedness and lofty disregard for details while paranoid shows acutely vigilant though biased attention (p. 159).
- Subtle paranoid tendencies recognizable from early childhood onwards may gather further coloration during adolescence before reaching full-blown adult picture; needs further investigation (Anthony 1981, Winnicott 1952) (p. 160).
- Lowenthal (1968) and Bergman (1971, 1978) suggest paranoid individuals often make better adjustments to aging than psychopathology suggests; fighting stance keeps them intact; failure is never their own; world taken on in single combat; life presents diverting struggle against dangerous external forces; little time for despair and depression (p. 160).
- Subtype question: Schneider (1958), Hamilton (1974, 1984), Shapiro (1965) propose two subtypes: weaker passive secretive cynically brooding; stronger actively defiant openly angry litigious; former has kinship with schizoid personality latter with narcissistic personality (p. 160).
- Alternative explanation may be varying level of social functioning manifesting by individual paranoid patients akin to Kernberg's (1975a) outline of different levels in narcissistic personality: certain paranoid better compensated achieve success in solitary professions more active open defiant; others weaker ego structures passive secretive unable to translate inner programs into action (p. 160).
- Yet another explanation may be that two paranoid styles are sex-related: silently resentful outcome more frequent among women and blatantly defiant picture seen more often in men; some support in literature (Bleuler 1908, Kraepelin 1905, 1921a) though matter far from settled (p. 160).
- Thomson: Co-authored phenomenological profiles of narcissistic personality that distinguish it from paranoid personality by its lack of pervasive mistrust, litigiousness, and self-righteous indignation (Akhtar and Thomson 1982).
- Winokur: Contributed to descriptions of paranoid disorders that focused on potential relationships with other conditions rather than specific phenomenological details (Winokur 1985).
- Kendler: Focused research on the potential relationship between paranoid personality disorder and paranoid schizophrenia or other paranoid disorders, rather than its phenomenological details (Kendler et al. 1984; Kendler and Grunberger 1982).
- Ndtei: Supported the distinction between reactive/acute paranoid delusions and paranoid personality disorder, noting that the latter is a nondelusional, stable state lacking a clear onset (Ndtei 1986).
- Wurmser (1981) points out three other differences between phobic and paranoid characters.
- Reid’s (1985) reminder notes that the person with paranoid personality characteristically lacks the chronicity of antisocial behavior—and the child to adult continuum—found in the psychopath.
- Conlon (1984) provided a mnemonic description of the “paranoid psychopath” characterized by traits including malicious, manipulative, mendacious, mendicant, meretricious, malignant, moralistic, mindless, and murderous behavior.
- Rettersol (1985) is identified in the source as a contributor to research focusing on paranoid disorders, which was distinguished from work focused on phenomenological details or specific paranoid disorder classifications.
- Slater and Roth (1977) offered a less clear description of paranoid personality than Hamilton (1974, 1984), while Stanton (1978) provided greater clarity than Winokur and Croewe (1975).

The psychoanalytic summary identifies nine features: evidence of severe sustained actual preoedipal trauma; lack of basic trust; aborted separation-individuation process and lack of self and object constancy; predominance of object-relations and dyadic conflicts over internalized structural conflicts; persistence of severe splitting with denial and projective identification; masochistic negative or perverse Oedipus complex outcome with intense unconscious beating fantasies sadomasochistic perversions or marked asexuality; syndrome of identity diffusion; uneven superego; peculiar corruption of autonomous ego functions resulting in narrow biased rigid hypervigilant cognitive style. Paranoid rage negativism hatred are viewed as affective concomitants of disturbed ego, measures to control external objects compensating for lack of inner object constancy, defending against passive surrender to love objects that stirs dread of incest homosexuality dissolution of self. The DSM section critiques DSM-III and DSM-III-R criteria. DSM-III listed paranoid personality disorder with criteria for pervasive unwarranted suspiciousness indicated by at least three items including expectation of trickery or harm, hypervigilance, guardedness or secretiveness, avoidance of accepting blame, questioning loyalty of others, intense narrowly focused searching for confirmation of bias, overconcern with hidden motives, pathological jealousy. Hypersensitivity indicated by at least two items: easily slighted, exaggeration of difficulties, readiness to counterattack, inability to relax. Restricted affectivity indicated by at least two items: cold unemotional appearance, pride in objectivity rationality, lack of true sense of humor, absence of passive soft tender sentimental feelings. DSM-III failed to include grandiosity, sexual difficulties, hidden inferiority, idiosyncratically moral attitudes, gullibility and naivete. Siever and Kendler (1987) noted many criteria were less specific; considerable overlap with other Axis II diagnoses existed; studies by Mellsop and Varghesi (1982) and Spitzer et al. (1979) suggested low reliability. DSM-III-R reduced from sixteen to seven items, making list more manageable; inclusion of bears grudges is improvement. However, both do not include superego unevenness, self-esteem defects, romantic sexual difficulties, core naive gullibility, or coexistence of mutually contradictory stances. The synthesis section proposes a composite profile (Table 6-1) organizing clinical features into six areas: self-concept, interpersonal relations, social adaptation, love and sexuality, ethics standards and ideals, cognitive style; each with overt and covert manifestations. Overt includes arrogant mistrustful suspicious of others, driven industrious successful in solitary professions, unromantic idiosyncratically moralistic sharply vigilant. Covert includes frightened timid gullible, chronically experiencing interpersonal difficulties in work, corruptible, vulnerable to erotomania and sadomasochistic perversions, cognitively unable to grasp totality of events in proper context. The overt-covert designations do not necessarily imply conscious or unconscious existence; they denote seemingly contradictory phenomenological aspects more or less easily discernible. This conceptualization underscores centrality of splitting and projective mechanisms; gives sounder developmental and dynamic underpinnings; prepares clinicians for mirror complementarity of the self that Bach (1977) noted. Risk exists that overt and covert get equated with conscious and unconscious topography; similar profiles faced questions about utilizability by dynamically unsophisticated psychiatrists (Frances et al. 1988); lack of actuarial data makes relative diagnostic weight difficult to ascertain. The profile remains superior to checklist methods because it includes both phenomenological and psychoanalytic observations, values depth and complexity over descriptive oversimplification, establishes connection between behavioral and psychostructural hence developmental aspects. It may lend itself better to prototypal model proposed by Frances and Widiger (1986) as only way to salvage categorical system; helps in meaningful differential diagnosis. The differential diagnosis section distinguishes paranoid personality disorder from paranoid schizophrenia (serious break with reality, bizarre delusions, auditory hallucinations, thought control symptoms, incongruous affect, gross behavioral peculiarities — none characteristic of paranoid personality), reactive or acute paranoid disorders (seen among immigrants, refugees, prisoners of war, inductees into military services, people leaving home for first time; delusions often comprehensible resolve quickly rarely become chronic; distinct from nondelusional stable state lacking clear onset), and paranoia or delusional disorder (difference largely quantitative per Bleuler 1908, Kraepelin 1905 1921, Leonhard 1959, Stanton 1978; some authors such as Munro 1987 consider them separate; paranoia has delusions while paranoid personality never does; kernel of truth greater in paranoid personality suspicions; accusations have more plausibility; attitudes diffuse and less encapsulated). The phobic character distinction notes that Fenichel (1946) coined the term for individuals whose reactive behavior limits itself to avoidance of situations originally desired for; Mackinnon and Michels (1971) recognized fearful avoidance as characterological defense; Stone (1980) described phobic-anxious personality. Phobic characters differ from paranoid personalities: they avoid situations not people; phobic feeling is It is a danger but I do not know why while paranoid feeling is It is a danger and I know why namely such and such is his or her intention; phobic lacks personalization and intentionality of outside menace. Leading affective reactions differ: in paranoid personality it is rage hatred contempt grudge; in phobic character it is anxiety guilty fears and somatic counterparts; paranoid reacts by attack or provocation of attack while phobic reacts by avoidance and flight. Phobic character does not display identity diffusion nor predominance of splitting over repression; should be regarded as higher level (Bak 1946) or neurotic character organization and ego-syntonic characterological counterpart (Akhtar 1986) of phobic neurosis proper; paranoid personality disorder shows lower level (Kernberg 1970) or borderline personality organization with pervasive splitting denial projective identification in setting of identity diffusion. The compulsive personality distinction notes similarities in rigidity, intense purposefulness, limited affective spontaneity, restricted sexuality, generalized difficulty with risk taking humor playfulness (Shapiro 1965); both dislike surprises prefer order predictability. However, compulsive knows pressure to conform emanates from within and regards moral dictates as unquestionably superior to instinctual desires; paranoid views such demands as originating externally and unjustified. Compulsive's rebellion is subtle unconscious disguised ego-alien (Fenichel 1945); paranoid consciously plots against imagined enemies. Cognitively, compulsive is overinclusive while paranoid has narrowly focused biased attention; compulsive is chronically indecisive and doubt-ridden while paranoid is smugly self-assured; interpersonally, compulsive capable of concern and mutuality while paranoid views everyone with jealous mistrust. Psychostructurally, compulsive displays higher level character organization with well-consolidated identity, predominance of repression over splitting, capacity for genuinely triadic reciprocal object relations, relatively intact autonomous ego functions, well-demarcated tripartite mind; paranoid displays lower level with identity diffusion, predominance of splitting over repression, impaired object relations, contaminated autonomous ego functions, mind riddled by loosely integrated unsynthesized sadomasochistic introjects. Developmentally, compulsive had successful separation-individuation process and acquired self-and object constancy; problems emanate largely from oedipal phase difficulties regressive substituted by anal phase metaphors; paranoid had much actual preoedipal trauma and unsuccessful separation-individuation, lacks self-and object constancy; superficial developmental history reveals more traumatized turbulent childhood in paranoid. The narcissistic personality distinction notes many similarities led Kernberg (1985) to declare distinction particularly difficult; both are ambitious haughty driven given to narcissistic rage when pride injured (Kohut 1972); both display predominance of splitting over repression though projective mechanisms more marked in paranoid; both show arrested separation-individuation, distorted Oedipus complex, less than optimal latency adolescent passage. Subtle identity diffusion often masked by overzealous yet shallow vocational commitment is discernible in both; superego unduly harsh uneven poorly demarcated from ego proper; cases with combined narcissistic and paranoid features may be more common than pure types (Kernberg 1985, Millon 1981). When overlap not marked, differences exist: narcissistic does not display pervasive mistrust litigiousness self-righteous indignation of paranoid; paranoid lacks charming social veneer seductiveness sexual promiscuity inordinate exploitativeness of narcissist; narcissist hides discomfort with spontaneity by crafty socialization while paranoid hides by angry negativism haughty aloofness brooding withdrawal. Cognitively, narcissist displays absentmindedness and lofty disregard for details while paranoid shows acutely vigilant though biased attention. The antisocial personality distinction notes overlap commented on by Jaspers (1949), Kraepelin (1921b); Conlon (1984) described paranoid psychopath" as malicious manipulative mendacious mendicant meretricious malignant moralistic mindless murderous plus sensitive secretive sentimental superior selfish sexually disturbed. Developmental psychostructural dynamic overlap exists but must keep Reid's (1985) reminder: paranoid lacks chronicity of antisocial behavior and child-to-adult continuum found in psychopath; acts consistent with projection paranoia without stimulating quality usually seen in antisocial personality. The comment section addresses two aspects: variations of paranoid symptomatology over lifespan and subclassification within paranoid personality disorder. Subtle paranoid tendencies recognizable from early childhood onwards may gather further coloration during adolescence before reaching full-blown adult picture (Anthony 1981, Winnicott 1952); needs further investigation. Lowenthal (1968) and Bergman (1971, 1978) suggest paranoid individuals often make better adjustments to aging than psychopathology suggests; fighting stance keeps them intact; failure is never their own; world taken on in single combat; life presents diverting struggle against dangerous external forces; little time for despair and depression. For subclassification, Schneider (1958), Hamilton (1974, 1984), Shapiro (1965) propose two subtypes: weaker passive secretive cynically brooding; stronger actively defiant openly angry litigious; former has kinship with schizoid personality latter with narcissistic personality. Alternative explanation may be varying level of social functioning manifesting by individual paranoid patients akin to Kernberg's (1975a) outline: certain paranoid better compensated achieve success in solitary professions more active open defiant; others weaker ego structures passive secretive unable to translate inner programs into action. Yet another explanation may be that two styles are sex-related: silently resentful outcome more frequent among women and blatantly defiant picture seen more often in men; some support in literature (Bleuler 1908, Kraepelin 1905, 1921a) though matter far from settled.

This cognitive rigidity serves as a protective mechanism against perceived threats, though it simultaneously limits the patient's capacity for social adaptation and emotional flexibility.
