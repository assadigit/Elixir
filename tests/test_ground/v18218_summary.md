# Summary: 1 - IDENTITY AND ITS ORIGINS

## Executive Summary

This chapter examines the concept of identity in psychoanalytic theory and its developmental origins. It begins by tracing the term from Tausk (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1919</em>) through Erikson's foundational work to later developmental frameworks. The chapter distinguishes sociocultural accounts of identity (Fromm 1955, Wheelis 1958, Lynd 1958, Lifton 1971, Lasch 1978, Kovel 1981) from developmental-psychoanalytic accounts. It then presents Erikson's definition of ego identity, Lichtenstein's philosophical phenomenological framework of identity theme and metamorphosis, Freud's implicit views on identity genesis, Fenichel's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>) early ego development, Greenacre's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) body-image continuum, Mahler's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) separation-individuation phases, Jacobson's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) five categories of identity development, Winnicott's true/false self distinction, Kernberg's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1967</em>) three-step identity formation process, Chasseguet-Smirgel's long/short path of ego-ideal formation, and Blos's (1962, 1965, 1967) adolescent identity consolidation. The chapter concludes by identifying seven characteristics of a solid identity and defining identity diffusion as the syndrome of disturbance across these areas.

## Key Takeaways

- The term identity was introduced into psychoanalytic literature by Tausk (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1919</em>), who examined how the child discovers his self and asserted that man must constantly find and experience himself anew throughout life.
- Sociocultural contributors (Fromm 1955, Wheelis 1958, Lynd 1958, Lifton 1971, Lasch 1978, Kovel 1981) use identity in its descriptive sense. They view the search for identity as a generational problem caused by the breakdown of past value systems.
- Freud, according to Erikson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), referred to identity only once, in his address to B'nai B'rith (Freud 1926a). The term remained largely unused because it is a two-sided concept with both intrapsychic and social ramifications.
- Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>) identified a tension in Erikson's work between ego identity (personal identity from self-objectivation) and existential identity (personal identity defined by "the relationship of each soul to its mere existence").
- Early analysts contributed concepts of rudimentary self-representations: Federn's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1952</em>) "ego feeling," Glover's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) "ego nuclei," and Spiegel's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1959</em>) "pooling" of isolated self-representations as underlying solid identity formation.
- Mahler (1958a,b, 1968, Mahler et al. 1975) distinguished psychological birth from biological birth. She postulated a sequence: autistic phase, symbiotic phase, differentiation phase, practicing phase, rapprochement phase, and the road to object constancy.
- Mahler stated two conditions for ego organization and identity: (1) enteroceptive-proprioceptive stimuli must not be so continual and intense as to prevent structure formation, requiring a shift from viscera-centered splanchnic innervation to the external rind of body ego; and (2) the mother must buffer and organize inner and other stimuli for the infant.
- Kernberg (1967, 1976, 1980b) proposed that ego identity development is based on internalization of early object relationships. The early ego must accomplish two tasks: differentiating self images from object images, and integrating self- and object images built under libidinal drives with those built under aggressive drives.
- The growing number of borderline and narcissistic patients has driven rising interest in identity and identity diffusion. Many analytic the oreticians (Jacobson 1964, Kernberg 1967) regard identity disturbances as a sine qua non of borderline and narcissistic character disorders.
- Erikson (1950a, 1956, 1958, 1959) resurrected the term in his contributions to character formation. Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) and Mahler (1958a,b, 1968) incorporated it into object relations and developmental models. Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1967</em>) emphasized the psychostructural and diagnostic significance of identity diffusion in severe character pathology.
- Erikson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1956</em>) defined ego identity as "both a persistent sameness within oneself (selfsameness) and a persistent sharing of some kind of essential character with others." He later dropped the prefix ego to accommodate Hartmann's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) differentiation between ego and self.
- Erikson emphasized adolescence as the key period for identity consolidation, a view shared by Eissler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) and enriched by Blos (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1962</em>). Failure in integrating past knowledge, present experiences, and future goals leads to a chaotic sense of personhood.
- Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>) proposed two opposing forces in identity: the identity principle (the perpetual drive to maintain historical existence and self-defined identity) and metamorphosis (the longing to abandon the human quality of identity). He sees identity and metamorphosis as "two limits of human experience incompatible with one another but complementary."
- Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) indicated that aggressive drive serves a growth-promoting function. Through frustrations, disappointments, and hostile experiences, the child learns the difference between wishful and realistic self- and object-images. Both loving and hostile components furnish the fuel for developing feelings of identity.
- Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1978</em>) distinguished adolescent identity crisis from identity diffusion, correlating the latter with borderline character organization. Kernberg (1980b) described middle age as adding renewed recognition of personal myths, deeper reworking of oedipal tasks, acceptance of limits of creativity, and a shift in time perspective.
- Fenichel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>) proposed that the image of self originates from two sources: direct awareness of inner experience and indirect perception of the bodily and mental self as an object. He gave equal importance to somatic foundations and identifications, emphasizing the "body image" as fundamental for ego development.
- Jacobson's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) contributions to identity development fall into five categories: (1) self- and object representations, (2) fused self-object representation as the first intrapsychic structure, (3) emergence of identity from primitive identifications facilitating further identifications, (4) the role of aggression in self-delineation, and (5) modifications of oedipal resolution and superego formation.
- The chapter concludes that a solid identity consists of seven characteristics: (1) sustained feeling of selfsameness displaying roughly similar character traits to varied others, (2) temporal continuity in self-experience, (3) genuineness and authenticity, (4) a realistic body image, (5) a sense of inner solidity and capacity for peaceful solitude, (6) subjective clarity regarding one's gender, and (7) inner solidarity with an ethnic group's ideals and a well-internalized conscience.
- Erikson identified four connotations of identity: a conscious sense of individual identity; an unconscious striving for continuity of personal character; a criterion for the silent doings of ego synthesis; and a maintenance of inner solidarity with group's ideals and identity.
- Freud rarely used the term identity in a technical manner. Of ninety-two times he used the word, ninety-one were colloquial or literal. His one technical use (Freud 1926a) was in passing.
- Jacobson argued that the superego is not simply the precipitate of aggressively tinged internalizations. It includes libidinally invested identifications, meaning loving aspects of relationships with both parents contribute to the superego, which deepens and maintains identity.
- Lichtenstein sees the symbiotic mother-child relationship as the beginning of identity formation. The mother imprints not an identity but an "identity theme" upon the infant. This theme is irreversible but capable of variations that distinguish human creativity from "a destiny neurosis."
- Greenacre (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) proposed a continuum of development from body image through self-image to identity. She emphasized the importance of habitual taking in through vision of opposite-sex genitals and the fusion of this part image with the subject's own body image.
- These sociocultural authors rest on two foundations: a stylistic foundation in the existential literature tradition (raising metaphysical questions like "Who am I?") and a content foundation from an overly sociological perspective, echoing Marx's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1867</em>) alienation and Durkheim's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1951</em>) concept of "anomie."
- Freud's implicit view of identity genesis accommodates somatic underpinnings, gender differences, early identifications, the Oedipus complex and its resolution, the ongoing synthetic function of the ego, and ethnic and moral dimensions.
- Winnicott's work provides the developmental basis for three aspects of identity: authenticity, "corporeality" (Khan 1983), and temporal continuity in the self. His most penetrating contribution concerns authenticity. The infant's spontaneous gestures emanate from the "true self." If the mother fails to decode the infant's overture, a "false self" emerges, which is defensive in nature and seeks to hide and protect the true self.
- Kernberg formulated a three-step process of identity formation: (1) introjection, whereby affectively charged attributes of others are internalized without full assimilation into the self-image; (2) identification, a less concrete, more role-oriented internalization situated "deeper" in the self-system; and (3) identity formation, whereby childhood identifications are synthesized into a harmonious gestalt and become "depersonified."
- Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) proposed two routes for the oedipal child: the "long path" (accepting the tragedy of chronological lag, forming ego ideal, sublimating pregenital libido, acquiring filiation and generational continuity) and the "short path" (bypassing the "fatal character of the Oedipus complex," fusing ego and ego ideal, failing to idealize the father).
- Blos identified three aspects of sexual identity consolidation: renunciation of primary love objects as sexual objects, reconciliation of activity and passivity, and resolution of bisexual identifications. For boys, this involves renouncing envy of the procreative mother and mastering negative oedipal tendencies. For girls, it involves giving up the illusory penis and deepening identification with the mother.
- Blos (1972, 1974, 1984) proposed a biphasic resolution of the Oedipus complex: the positive complex resolves in childhood, while the negative complex, especially in boys, resolves in adolescence. The ego ideal is "the heir of the negative Oedipus complex," guarded as a cherished personality attribute whose archaic origin lies in father attachment.
- Mahler's "self-constancy" consists of awareness of being a separate entity and a beginning awareness of gender-defined identity. The former begins in the differentiation subphase and consolidates at the termination of the rapprochement subphase (approximately two years). The latter emanates from constitutionally predestined gender-defined differences, further elaborated in the phallic-oedipal phase.
- Jacobson proposed a developmental sequence: fused self-object representation evolves through primary libidinal identifications; differentiation begins; recognition of painful experiences leads to denial, splitting, and projection; integration of "good" and "bad" representations yields a realistic self-representation; identity comes into existence; discovery of identity facilitates more complex interactions with "total" parental objects.
- Winnicott emphasized that the infant is a "psychosomatic being." In individuals with a false self, a mind-body split occurs: the mind becomes the locus of identity, and the body is relegated to the status of a disowned vestige.
- Blos (1962, 1965, 1967) described the second individuation process of adolescence as emotional disengagement from internalized infantile objects, producing "radical alterations" in ego structure. The superego loses power, specificity, and rigidity, yielding greater inner constancy and abstract morality independent of childhood parents.
- Winnicott (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1960</em>) described the earliest ontogenetic roots of temporal continuity: "All the processes of a live infant constitute a going-on-being." Any impingement or failure of adaptation breaks up the going-on-being, interfering with the infant's natural tendency to become an integrated unit with past, present, and future.
- Identity diffusion comprises disturbances in these seven areas of functioning. The chapter positions identity as emerging from earliest infant-mother interactions, gaining structure from primitive introjections, refining through differentiation and selective identifications, acquiring filiation and temporality through the Oedipus complex, and arriving at its more or less final shape through further synthesis, individuation, and renunciation of negative oedipal tendencies during adolescence.
- - Stoller: Made significant contributions to the specific area of gender identity consolidation (1968, 1972).
- - Galenson and Roiphe: Contributed to the specific area of gender identity consolidation (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>).
- - Meyer: Contributed to the specific area of gender identity consolidation (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1980</em>).
- - Loewenstein: Co-authored metapsychological work with Hartmann and Kris (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1946</em>), which Jacobson integrated with her findings on affective disorders and severe adolescent identity disturbances.
- - Spitz: Offered a developmental hypothesis (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1957</em>) that Jacobson combined with her findings on affective disorders and identity disturbances.
- - Klein: His work (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1948</em>) was integrated by Kernberg with Erikson, Jacobson, and Mahler to formulate a three-step process of identity formation.
- - Loewald: Pointed out (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1951</em>) the necessity for a growing toddler of a stable relationship with the father to protect against the threat of precompetitive libidinal tie, highlighting the father's role in psychic structuring and identity consolidation.
- - Searles: Discussed (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1960</em>) the longing to abandon the human quality of identity, a force juxtaposed to the driving pressure of the "identity theme."

## Thorough Summary

This chapter begins by placing the problem of identity in a clinical setting. It deals with borderline and narcissistic patients. These patients have poorly formed identities. They show contradictions in self-images, aspirations, and relationships. The author says the literature on identity disturbance is scattered and not well organized. The author aims to bring together various writings on the topic. Tausk (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1919</em>) introduced the term "identity" into psychoanalytic literature. He studied how a child discovers the self. Freud, according to Erikson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), used the word "identity" only once, in his address to B'nai B'rith (Freud 1926a). The term has two sides: intrapsychic and social. This dual nature may explain its limited acceptance. Erikson (1950a, 1956, 1958, 1959) brought the term back in his work on character formation. Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) and Mahler (1958a,b, 1968) added it to object relations and developmental models. Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1967</em>) stressed the psychostructural and diagnostic importance of identity diffusion in severe character pathology. In 1980, he said: "it is the presence or absence of identity diffusion that most clearly differentiates borderline from non-borderline conditions." Erikson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1956</em>) defined ego identity as "both a persistent sameness within oneself (selfsameness) and a persistent sharing of some kind of essential character with others." He listed four connotations. First, a conscious sense of individual identity. Second, an unconscious drive for continuity of personal character. Third, a standard for the silent work of ego synthesis. Fourth, keeping inner unity with a group's ideals and identity. Erikson stressed adolescence as the key period for identity consolidation. Eissler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) agreed. Blos (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1962</em>) added to this view. Sociocultural contributors (Fromm 1955, Wheelis 1958, Lynd 1958, Lifton 1971, Lasch 1978, Kovel 1981) use identity in a descriptive way. They see the search for identity as a generational problem. It is caused by the breakdown of old value systems. Their work rests on two foundations. One is stylistic, from the existential literature tradition, raising metaphysical questions. The other is about content, from a too-sociological view. It echoes Marx's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1867</em>) alienation and Durkheim's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1951</em>) "anomie." Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>) found a tension in Erikson's work between ego identity and existential identity. He proposed two opposing forces. One is the identity principle, the constant drive to keep historical existence. The other is metamorphosis, the desire to give up the human quality of identity. He sees the symbiotic mother-child relationship as the start of identity formation. The mother imprints an "identity theme" on the infant. The developmental roots of identity start with Freud's implicit views. These include somatic bases, gender differences, early identifications, the Oedipus complex, the ongoing synthetic function of the ego, and ethnic and moral dimensions. Fenichel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>) built on Freud's ideas. He proposed that the self-image comes from direct awareness of inner experience and indirect perception of the bodily and mental self as an object. He gave equal weight to somatic bases and identifications. He stressed the "body image" as basic. Other early analysts added ideas about basic self-representations. Federn (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1952</em>) proposed "ego feeling." Glover (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) proposed "ego nuclei." Spiegel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1959</em>) proposed "pooling" of isolated self-representations. Greenacre (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) proposed a continuum from body image to self-image to identity. He stressed the importance of habitual visual taking in of opposite-sex genitals and their fusion with the subject's own body image. Mahler (1958a,b, 1968, Mahler et al. 1975) separated psychological birth from biological birth. She set out a sequence of phases: the autistic phase, the symbiotic phase, the differentiation phase, the practicing phase, the rapprochement phase, and the road to object constancy. She stated two conditions for ego organization. First, enteroceptive-proprioceptive stimuli must not be so constant and intense that they block structure formation. A shift is needed from viscera-centered splanchnic innervation to the external rind of body ego. Second, the mother must buffer and organize inner and other stimuli. "Self-constancy" means awareness of being a separate entity and a first awareness of gender-defined identity. Jacobson's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) contributions fall into five areas. They are: self- and object representations, fused self-object representation, the rise of identity from primitive identifications, the role of aggression in self-delineation, and changes in oedipal resolution and superego formation. Her developmental sequence moves from fused self-object representation through differentiation, recognition of painful experiences, integration of "good" and "bad" representations, to a realistic self-representation from which identity emerges. Winnicott's work gives the developmental basis for authenticity, "corporeality" (Khan 1983), and temporal continuity. His deepest contribution is about the true self versus the false self. If the mother fails to decode the infant's overture, the child develops an outwardly compliant but inauthentic self. The false self is defensive. It tries to hide and protect the true self. In people with a false self, a mind-body split occurs. The mind becomes the home of identity. The body becomes a disowned leftover. Winnicott (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1960</em>) described the earliest roots of temporal continuity: "All the processes of a live infant constitute a going-on-being." Kernberg (1967, 1976, 1980b) set out a three-step process: introjection, identification, and identity formation. The early ego must separate self images from object images. It must also integrate images built under libidinal drives with those built under aggressive drives. Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) proposed two paths for the oedipal child. The "long path" leads to ego ideal formation, sublimation, and filiation. The "short path" leads to a perverse character where ego and ego ideal are fused. Blos (1962, 1965, 1967) described the second individuation process of adolescence. He also described a biphasic resolution of the Oedipus complex. The ego ideal is "the heir of the negative Oedipus complex." The chapter ends by listing seven traits of a solid identity. First, a lasting feeling of selfsameness, showing roughly similar character traits to varied others. Second, temporal continuity in self-experience. Third, genuineness and authenticity. Fourth, a realistic body image. Fifth, a sense of inner solidity and the ability for peaceful solitude. Sixth, subjective clarity about one's gender. Seventh, inner unity with an ethnic group's ideals and a well-internalized conscience. Identity diffusion means disturbances in these areas. Identity comes from the earliest infant-mother interactions. It gains structure from primitive introjections. It is refined through differentiation and selective identifications. It gains filiation and temporality through the Oedipus complex. It reaches its more or less final shape through further synthesis, individuation, and giving up negative oedipal tendencies during adolescence.

## Key Distinctions

- Introjection versus identification (Kernberg): Introjection involves internalizing affectively charged, specific attributes of others without full assimilation into the self-image. It produces an "organized cluster of memory traces" that feels like a "foreign body" in the self and is often preconscious. Identification is less concrete, more role-oriented, and situated "deeper" in the self-system. Identifications do not feel foreign and require a more sophisticated ego with more advanced cognitive and perpetual abilities.
- The "long path" versus the "short path" of ego-ideal formation (Chasseguet-Smirgel): The long path involves accepting the tragedy of chronological lag between erotic longing and genital capacity, sublimating pregenital libido, and acquiring filiation and generational continuity. The short path bypasses the "fatal character of the Oedipus complex," fuses ego and ego ideal, and fails to idealize the father. The short path leads to a perverse character organization.
- Ego identity versus existential identity (Lichtenstein, citing Erikson): Ego identity is personal identity resulting from self-objectivation. Existential identity is personal identity defined by "the relationship of each soul to its mere existence." Lichtenstein argues this tension must be accepted as inherent in the concept of identity.
- True self versus false self (Winnicott): The true self emanates from the infant's spontaneous gestures and personal ideas. The false self emerges when the mother fails to decode the infant's overture and replaces it with her own gesture. The false self is defensive, seeking to hide and protect the true self. In false-self individuals, a mind-body split occurs, relegating the body to a disowned vestige.
- Adolescent identity crisis versus identity diffusion (Kernberg 1978): The chapter distinguishes the usual identity crisis of adolescence from the syndrome of identity diffusion. Identity diffusion is correlated with a borderline level of character organization and is more severe and persistent than the normative adolescent crisis.


## Theoretical Tensions

- The two-sidedness of the concept of identity: The chapter explicitly identifies identity as a "two-sided term with intrapsychic as well as social ramifications" and an "admittedly Janusfaced concept." Erikson (1958) stated he can make identity more explicit only by approaching it from a variety of angles and "by letting the term identity speak for itself in a number of connotations." Lichtenstein (1961) argues that the conflict between identity as self-objectivation and identity as pure existential awareness "has to be accepted as such."
- The tension between identity and metamorphosis (Lichtenstein): Lichtenstein proposes identity and metamorphosis as "two limits of human experience incompatible with one another but complementary." Human life exists in an oscillation between these two extremes. The loss of identity produces confusion and anguish, yet can also be experienced as liberation and ecstasy. Sexual union demonstrates both forces: an individual becomes "an instrument for the fulfillment of another one's needs" while also surrendering identity altogether.
- The relative significance of infantile versus adolescent phases in identity consolidation: The chapter notes that areas needing further clarification include "the relative significance of infantile and adolescent phases in identity consolidation." Erikson and Mahler both emphasized adolescenceo fill these lacunae.
- The tension between the existential-philosophical and the developmental-empirical approaches: The sociocultural authors (Fromm, Wheelis, Lynd, Lifton, Lasch, Kovel) raise questions that "appear answerable only by a god," making their queries "more metaphysical than psychological." Their content emanates from "an overly sociological perspective." Lichtenstein's work is described as "largely in the philosophical phenomenological realm," yet his ideas about "identity theme" and early mother-child interactions have "significant bearing upon the ontogenetic development of personal identity."
