# Summary: 1 - IDENTITY AND ITS ORIGINS

## Executive Summary

The chapter addresses identity diffusion as a central problem in borderline and narcissistic character disorders, citing Jacobson (1964) and Kernberg (1967) who regard identity disturbances as a sine qua non of these conditions.
The existing literature on clinical phenomena associated with identity disturbance is described as scattered and ill-synthesized, motivating the chapter's integrative purpose.
The chapter aims to bring together various writings on identity and to comment upon developmental processes contributing to the origins of personal identity.
The author identifies a need to clarify the very concept of identity, noting its admittedly ambiguous place in psychoanalytic theory.
The chapter opens with a Walt Whitman epigraph from 'Song of Myself' listing multiple social roles, framing identity as multifaceted and encompassing diverse ranks, religions, and occupations. Tausk (1919) introduced the term identity into psychoanalytic literature.
Freud used the term identity technically only once in a 1926 address to B’nai B’rith.
Erikson defined ego identity as both self-sameness and shared essential character with others.
Kernberg identified identity diffusion as the key differentiator for borderline conditions.
Lichtenstein proposed 'identity theme' and 'metamorphosis' as opposing forces in identity.

- The epigraph frames the speaker as a 'novice beginning yet experient of myriads of seasons' who is 'of every hue and caste... of every rank and religion.'
- Fenichel (1937) argued that "the sum of representations of his own body and its organs which arises in the process, the so-called 'body image,' is of fundamental importance for the further development of his ego," and Fenichel (1945) added that "into the formation of every ego there have gone many identifications," establishing equal weight for somatic foundations and identifications in identity origins.
- Spiegel (1959) contributed the concept of "the 'pooling' of isolated self-representation as underlying solid identity formation," while Federn's (1952) "ego feeling" and Glover's (1950) "ego nuclei" represented earlier approximations of rudimentary self-representations that form identity's building blocks.
- Greenacre (1958a,b) established a "continuum of development from body image through self-image to identity" and emphasized "the importance of the habitual taking in through vision of the opposite-sex genitals and the fusion of this part image with that of the subject's own body," providing both preformation and epigenesis in identity formation.
- Mahler (1958a,b, 1968) delineated a specific sequence: "the autistic phase...the symbiotic phase...the differentiation phase...the practicing phase...and the rapprochement phase," culminating at approximately two years of age when "self constancy" or "a stable sense of one's unique and separate personhood comes into being."
- Mahler specified two conditions for ego organization: "(1) the enteroceptive-proprioceptive stimuli must not be so continual and so intense as to prevent structure formation; thus a shift from viscera-centered splanchnic innervation to the external rind of body ego is essential for self boundary formation, and (2) the mother must be able to buffer and organize inner and other stimuli for the infant."
- Winnicott (1949) distinguished the "true self" emanating from "the aliveness of body tissues and working of body functions" from a "false self" produced when the mother fails to decode the infant's overture, and identified the earliest roots of temporal continuity in the infant's "going-on-being," which any impingement can break.
- Jacobson (1964) organized her contributions into five categories: "(1) the concept of self-and object representations, (2) the notion of a fused self-object representation as the first intrapsychic structure, (3) the emergence of identity from primitive identifications and its, in turn, facilitating further, more refined identifications, (4) the role of aggression in self-delineation, and (5) the modifications of the theory of oedipal resolution and superego formation."
- Kernberg formulated a three-step process: "The first step in the process is introjection...The second step is identification, which implies a less concrete and more role-oriented internalization of significant others in relationship to oneself...The third step is identity formation, whereby the childhood identifications are synthesized into a harmonious gestalt."
- Kernberg (1978) "described the distinctions between the adolescent identity crisis and the syndrome of identity diffusion, which he correlated with a borderline level of character organization," while Kernberg (1980b) "delineated the new dimensions that middle age adds to the consolidation of ego identity," including "a renewed recognition of personal myths, a deeper reworking of oedipal tasks, an acceptance of the limits of one's creativity."
- Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) distinguished the "long path" (accepting chronological lag, forming ego ideal, acquiring "a sense of filiation and generational continuity") from the "short path" leading to perverse character, where "the 'fatal character of the Oedipus complex' is bypassed; sublimation is circumvented."
- Blos (1984) proposed that "the adult ego ideal is the heir of the negative Oedipus complex," identifying three areas for identity consolidation: the second individuation process, consolidation of sexual identity through renunciation of primary love objects and resolution of bisexual identifications, and formation of ego ideal from precompetitive libidinal attachment to the father.
- The chapter's conclusion enumerates seven characteristics of solid identity: "(1) a sustained feeling of selfsameness displaying roughly similar character traits to varied others, (2) temporal continuity in the self-experience, (3) genuineness and authenticity, (4) a realistic body image, (5) a sense of inner solidity and the associated capacity for peaceful solitude, (6) subjective clarity regarding one's gender, and (7) an inner solidarity with an ethnic group's ideals and a well-internalized conscience."
- Lichtenstein (1961) proposed that "the mother imprints upon the infant not an identity, but an 'identity theme.' This theme is irreversible, but it is capable of variations, variations that spell the difference between human creativity and 'a destiny neurosis,'" and juxtaposed this with "metamorphosis," the longing to abandon identity entirely.
- Loewald (1951) and Mahler (1975) "pointed out the necessity for a growing toddler of a stable relationship with the father to protect himself against the threat of this precompetitive, libidinal tie, thus highlighting the significant role of the father in psychic structuring and identity consolidation."
- Gender identity consolidation specifically "has received significant contributions from Stoller (1968, 1972), Galenson and Roiphe (1971), and Meyer (1980)," building on Mahler's observation that self-constancy includes "a beginning awareness of a gender-defined identity" emanating from "constitutionally predestined gender-defined differences in the behavior of boys and girls."
- The sociocultural authors (Fromm, Wheelis, Lynd, Lifton, Lasch, Kovel) rest on two foundations: a stylistic one rooted in "the poignantly subjective and almost solipsistically experiential tradition of the existential literature" and a content-based one that is "an overly sociological perspective," with hypotheses reminding one of "the Marxist depictions of the individual's alienation" and "the Durkheimian concept of individual 'anomie.'"
- - Loewenstein: Co-authored the metapsychological work (Hartmann, Kris, and Loewenstein 1946) that Jacobson integrated into her framework for understanding identity and affective disorders.
- - Spitz: Contributed a developmental hypothesis (1957) that informed Jacobson's integration of findings on affective disorders and adolescent identity disturbances.
- - Klein: Their work (1948) was integrated by Kernberg, alongside Erikson, Jacobson, and Mahler, to formulate a three-step process of identity formation.
- - Searles: Addressed the second force juxtaposed to the driving pressure of identity—the longing to abandon the human quality of identity (1960).
- - Khan: Provided the developmental basis for authenticity, corporeality (a deeply assimilated and personalized bodily foundation for self-experience), and the experience of temporal continuity in the self.

## Thorough Summary

The chapter opens with a Walt Whitman epigraph from 'Song of Myself' in which the speaker inhabits twelve distinct social roles, presenting identity as plural—encompassing 'every hue and caste' and 'every rank and religion'—and describing themselves simultaneously as 'A novice beginning yet experient of myriads of seasons,' thereby combining beginner and experienced perspectives. This Whitmanian vision of multiplicity frames the clinical problem that motivates the chapter: a growing number of borderline and narcissistic patients presenting to psychoanalysts and other psychotherapists with poorly synthesized identities alongside contradictions in three named domains—self-images, aspirations, and interpersonal relationships. Jacobson (1964) and Kernberg (1967), identified as representatives of 'many analytic theoreticians' rather than isolated positions, hold that identity disturbance is a sine qua non of borderline and narcissistic character disorders. The author groups these two figures together to signal that they embody a broader school, yet notes a gap in the existing literature: writings on clinical phenomena associated with identity disturbance remain scattered and ill-synthesized. Accordingly, the chapter pursues two stated aims—first, to bring together the various writings on identity into a coherent account; second, to comment upon the developmental processes contributing to the origins of personal identity. Before addressing these aims, however, the author insists that a clarification of the very concept of identity is necessary at the outset, explicitly acknowledging that identity occupies an 'admittedly ambiguous' position within psychoanalytic theory. The term 'identity diffusion,' used alongside 'identity' as a paired concept, signals that diffusion is the pathological or disorganized form of identity, and the clinical population of concern is specifically 'borderline and narcissistic' patients rather than all psychiatric or psychotherapeutic populations. This ambiguity in the concept of identity becomes historically intelligible when one traces the term's contested entry into psychoanalytic discourse. Tausk (1919) introduced 'identity' into psychoanalytic literature, focusing on self-discovery and continuous self-experience, while Freud used the term technically only once, in a 1926 address to B'nai B'rith. The term's dual intrapsychic and social nature limited its early acceptance within the discipline. Erikson (1950s) subsequently revived the term, and Jacobson and Mahler integrated it into personality models, broadening its theoretical reach. Kernberg (1967) then highlighted the diagnostic role of identity diffusion in borderline personality organization, and by 1980 he had stated that identity diffusion differentiates borderline from non-borderline conditions, cementing the concept's clinical utility. Building on Erikson's revival, his elaboration of the concept introduced a structured theoretical architecture. He defined ego identity as self-sameness and shared essential character with others, and later dropped the 'ego' prefix to align with Hartmann's ego-self differentiation. Erikson listed four connotations of identity: conscious sense, unconscious striving, ego synthesis criterion, and group solidarity. Crucially, he emphasized adolescence as the period for identity consolidation—a position supported by Eissler and Blos—arguing that adolescents integrate past, present, and future into a stable continuum for personal cohesion, and that failure in this integration leads to chaotic personhood both subjectively and socially. Yet unresolved issues persisted within his framework: the relationship between infantile and adolescent phases, the distinction between crisis and diffusion, and the correlations with pathology all remained open. Erikson's work thus extended beyond the intrapsychic toward sociocultural determinants and personal childhood origins. In contrast to Erikson's primarily intrapsychic and developmental emphasis, a constellation of authors—Fromm, Wheelis, Lynd, Lifton, Lasch, and Kovel—focused on sociocultural determinants, viewing the identity search as a generational problem precipitated by value system breakdown. Their approach rests on two foundations: an existential stylistic foundation that poses metaphysical questions such as 'Who am I?', and a content foundation reflecting Marxist alienation and Durkheimian anomie. Lichtenstein noted a productive tension within Erikson's own work between ego identity and existential identity, observing that Erikson accepted this ambiguity by employing biographic, pathographic, and theoretical angles simultaneously rather than resolving the tension analytically. This dialectical movement between identity and its dissolution finds its most explicit formulation in Lichtenstein's proposal of the 'identity principle'—the drive to maintain historical and self-created identity—which he positioned against 'metamorphosis,' the longing to abandon identity. The mother-child symbiosis initiates identity formation via an 'identity theme,' establishing the earliest relational ground upon which a stable sense of self is built. Identity and metamorphosis are thereby framed as incompatible but complementary limits of human experience: one pulls toward coherence and continuity, the other toward dissolution and renewal. Together, these themes—clinical presentation, historical conceptual development, Eriksonian elaboration, sociocultural-existential expansion, and the identity-metamorphosis dialectic—constitute the intellectual architecture within which the chapter's title 'IDENTITY AND ITS ORIGINS' promises to address both the nature of identity and its developmental origins.

## Key Distinctions

The distinction between 'identity' as a general concept and 'identity diffusion' as the specific pathological process is central to the chapter's clinical framing. Identity refers to the organized self-structure; identity diffusion refers to its disorganized, poorly synthesized form seen in borderline and narcissistic patients.
The distinction between identity as studied in psychoanalytic theory (where it occupies an 'admittedly ambiguous' place) and identity as observed clinically in specific patient populations. The author signals that theoretical clarity must precede clinical discussion.
The distinction between 'poorly synthesized identities' (the patient's internal state) and 'contradictions in self-images, aspirations, and interpersonal relationships' (the observable clinical presentation). The first describes the structural deficit; the second describes its manifestations across three specific domains.
The distinction between identity disturbance as a sine qua non (indispensable condition) of borderline/narcissistic disorders versus the broader 'scattered' literature on clinical phenomena associated with such disturbance. The former is a diagnostic criterion position; the latter is a descriptive research field.
Ego identity vs. Existential identity: Lichtenstein distinguishes personal identity from self-objectivation vs. relationship to mere existence.
Identity theme vs. Metamorphosis: Lichtenstein contrasts the drive to maintain identity with the longing to abandon it.
Adolescent crisis vs. Identity diffusion: Erikson’s normal adolescent crisis is distinct from pathological identity diffusion.
Sociocultural vs. Personal childhood origins: Erikson’s work extended in two divergent directions regarding identity determinants.
Intrapsychic vs. Social ramifications: The dual nature of identity hindered its early psychoanalytic acceptance.


## Theoretical Tensions

Tension between the clinical urgency (growing patient populations needing help) and the theoretical inadequacy (identity's 'admittedly ambiguous' place in psychoanalytic theory). The clinical demand for understanding identity outpaces the theoretical framework available to explain it.
Tension between the integrative aim (bringing together scattered writings) and the acknowledged fragmentation of the existing literature. The author positions the chapter as a synthesis effort while simultaneously acknowledging that the field has not yet achieved coherence.
Tension between the Whitman epigraph (identity as richly plural, encompassing 'every hue and caste,' multiple simultaneous roles) and the clinical picture described in the text (identity as poorly synthesized, contradictory, diffused). The epigraph presents multiplicity as wholeness; the clinical description presents it as pathology.
Identity vs. Metamorphosis: Lichtenstein presents these as incompatible but complementary limits of human experience, creating an oscillation in life.
Existential vs. Sociological foundations: The sociocultural authors combine existential stylistic questions with sociological content on alienation and anomie.
Ego identity vs. Existential identity: Lichtenstein identifies a persistent tension between self-objectivation and pure existential awareness in Erikson’s work.
Ambiguity vs. Explicitness: Erikson accepted the inherent ambiguity of identity, choosing to let the term speak for itself rather than forcing explicit definitions.
