# Summary: 1 - IDENTITY AND ITS ORIGINS

## Executive Summary

This chapter addresses the concept of identity and its developmental origins in psychoanalytic theory. The author traces the term "identity" from its introduction by Tausk (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1919</em>) through Erikson's (1950s) revival of the concept. Erikson defined ego identity as a persistent sameness within oneself and a persistent sharing of essential character with others. The chapter then follows two extensions of Erikson's work: sociocultural determinants explored by Fromm (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1955</em>), Wheelis (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lynd (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lifton (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>), Lasch (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1978</em>), and Kovel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1981</em>), and personal childhood origins examined by Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>), Fenichel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>), Greenacre (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Mahler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>), Winnicott, Kernberg (1967, 1976, 1980b), Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984), and Blos (1962, 1965, 1967). Lichtenstein proposed "identity theme" and "metamorphosis" as two opposing forces in identity formation. Jacobson organized development into five categories, and Kernberg formulated a three-step process of introjection, identification, and identity formation. The chapter concludes by identifying seven characteristics of a solid identity and defining identity diffusion as disturbance in these areas. The author argues that identity is a two-sided term with intrapsychic and social ramifications, and that its origins span from earliest mother-infant interactions through adolescent consolidation.

## Key Takeaways

- Tausk (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1919</em>) introduced the term "identity" into psychoanalytic literature and asserted that man must constantly find and experience himself anew throughout life.
- Erikson emphasized adolescence as the key period for consolidating identity, a view shared by Eissler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) and enriched by Blos (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1962</em>).
- Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1967</em>) emphasized the psychostructural and diagnostic significance of identity diffusion in patients with severe character pathology. In 1980, he declared that identity diffusion most clearly differentiates borderline from non-borderline conditions.
- Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>) identified two views in Erikson's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) work: ego identity (personal identity from self-objectivation) and existential identity (personal identity defined by the "relationship of each soul to its mere existence").
- Mahler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) distinguished the psychological birth of the infant from biological birth. She proposed phases: autistic, symbiotic, differentiation, practicing, rapprochement, and the road to object constancy.
- Mahler specified two conditions for identity: (1) enteroceptive-proprioceptive stimuli must not be so continual and intense as to prevent structure formation, requiring a shift from viscera-centered splanchnic innervation to the external rind of body ego; (2) the mother must buffer and organize inner and other stimuli for the infant.
- Federn's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1952</em>) "ego feeling" and Glover's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) "ego nuclei" approximate rudimentary self-representations forming the building blocks of identity. Spiegel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1959</em>) described the "pooling" of isolated self-representation as underlying solid identity formation.
- Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1978</em>) described the distinctions between adolescent identity crisis and identity diffusion. Kernberg (1980b) delineated the new dimensions that middle age adds to the consolidation of ego identity.
- Erikson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1956</em>) defined ego identity as "both a persistent sameness within oneself (selfsameness) and a persistent sharing of some kind of essential character with others." He later dropped the prefix "ego" to accommodate Hartmann's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) differentiation between ego and self.
- Fromm (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1955</em>), Wheelis (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lynd (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lifton (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>), Lasch (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1978</em>), and Kovel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1981</em>) addressed sociocultural determinants of identity. They view the search for identity as a generational problem caused by the breakdown of past value systems.
- Lichtenstein proposed two opposing forces: the "identity principle" (the perpetual drive to maintain historical existence and self-created identity) and the longing to abandon identity, which he termed "metamorphosis." He sees identity and metamorphosis as two limits of human experience incompatible with one another but complementary.
- Fenichel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>) suggested the image of self originates from two sources: direct awareness of inner experience and indirect perception of bodily and mental self as an object. He gave equal importance to somatic foundations and identifications.
- Lichtenstein saw the symbiotic mother-child relationship as the beginning of identity formation. The mother imprints an "identity theme" on the infant, which is irreversible but capable of variations.
- Jacobson combined findings from treatment of affective disorders and severe adolescent identity disturbances with the metapsychological works of Freud, Fenichel, Hartmann, Mahler, and Spitz.
- The conclusion identifies seven characteristics of a solid identity: (1) sustained feeling of selfsameness displaying roughly similar character traits to varied others; (2) temporal continuity in self-experience; (3) genuineness and authenticity; (4) realistic body image; (5) inner solidity and capacity for peaceful solitude; (6) subjective clarity regarding gender; (7) inner solidarity with an ethnic group's ideals and a well-internalized conscience.
- Erikson identified four connotations of identity: a conscious sense of individual identity; an unconscious striving for continuity of personal character; a criterion for the silent doings of ego synthesis; and maintenance of inner solidarity with group's ideals and identity.
- Greenacre (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) proposed a continuum from body image through self-image to identity. She emphasized the importance of habitual taking in of opposite-sex genitals and their fusion with the subject's own body image.
- Kernberg (1967, 1976, 1980b) formulated a three-step process of identity formation: (1) introjection, whereby affectively charged attributes of others are internalized without full assimilation; (2) identification, a less concrete and more role-oriented internalization; (3) identity formation, whereby childhood identifications are synthesized into a harmonious gestalt.
- Kernberg distinguished adolescent identity crisis from identity diffusion, correlating the latter with borderline character organization. He also described middle age as adding renewed recognition of personal myths, deeper reworking of oedipal tasks, acceptance of limits of creativity, and a shift in time perspective.
- Blos identified three aspects of sexual identity consolidation: (1) renunciation of parents as sexual objects; (2) reconciliation of activity and passivity; (3) resolution of bisexual identifications.
- Blos (1972, 1974, 1984) proposed that the adult ego ideal is "the heir of the negative Oedipus complex," guarded as a cherished personality attribute whose archaic origin lies in father attachment.
- Stoller (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1968</em>) contributed to the specific area of gender identity consolidation, along with Galenson and Roiphe (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>) and Meyer (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1980</em>).
- Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) organized identity development into five categories: (1) self-and object representations; (2) fused self-object representation as the first intrapsychic structure; (3) identity emerging from primitive identifications and facilitating further refined identifications; (4) the role of aggression in self-delineation; (5) modifications of oedipal resolution and superego formation.
- Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) proposed two routes for the oedipal child: the "long path" (accepting the chronological lag, forming ego ego ideal, enhancing delayed gratification) and the "short path" (bypassing the Oedipal tragedy, leading to perverse character organization with fused ego and ego ideal).
- Freud's implicit view of identity accommodates somatic underpinnings, gender differences, early identifications, the Oedipus complex and its resolution, the ongoing synthetic function of the ego, and ethnic and moral dimensions.
- Blos (1962, 1965, 1967) described the second individuation process of adolescence, requiring emotional disengagement from internalized infantile objects, producing radical alterations in ego structure and a more autonomous superego.
- Disturbances in these seven areas comprise the syndrome of identity diffusion.
- The chapter opens with a Whitman quote emphasizing the multiplicity of identity across ranks, religions, and roles.
- The chapter notes that identity is a "two-sided term" with intrapsychic as well as social ramifications, which contributed to its limited acceptance in early psychoanalytic literature.
- Winnicott's work addresses three aspects of identity formation: authenticity, corporeality (a deeply assimilated bodily foundation for self-experience), and temporal continuity in the self. The true self comes from "the aliveness of body tissues and working of body functions."
- - **Loewenstein**: Contributed metapsychological frameworks (Hartmann, Kris, and Loewenstein 1946) that Jacobson integrated with developmental hypotheses from Mahler and Spitz to understand identity disturbances and affective disorders.
- - **Klein**: His 1948 work was integrated by Kernberg with Erikson, Jacobson, and Mahler to formulate a three-step process of identity formation.
- - **Loewald**: Highlighted the significant role of the father in psychic structuring and identity consolidation, emphasizing the necessity for a stable paternal relationship to protect the toddler against precompetitive libidinal threats.
- - **Searles**: Contributed to the understanding of a second force juxtaposed to the identity theme: the longing to abandon the human quality of identity.
- - **Khan**: Provided the developmental basis for authenticity, "corporeality" (a deeply assimilated and personalized bodily foundation for self-experience), and the experience of temporal continuity in the self.

## Thorough Summary

This chapter looks at identity problems in borderline and narcissistic patients. These patients have mixed-up identities. They show contradictions in how they see themselves, what they want, and how they relate to others. The word "identity" was first used by Tausk in 1919. He studied how a child finds its self. Freud used the term only once, in a speech to B'nai B'rith. Erikson brought the term back in his work on character formation (1950, 1956, 1958, 1959). He defined ego identity as a steady sense of self and a shared essential character with others. He listed four meanings: 1. A conscious sense of being an individual. 2. An unconscious drive to keep personal character continuous. 3. A standard for the ego's quiet work of synthesis. 4. Keeping inner unity with group ideals and identity. Erikson said adolescence is the time to solidify identity. Eissler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) agreed, and Blos (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1962</em>) added to this view. Later, Kernberg (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1967</em>) focused on the structural and diagnostic importance of identity diffusion in borderline patients. The chapter follows two paths from Erikson's work. One path focuses on social and cultural causes. Fromm (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1955</em>), Wheelis (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lynd (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>), Lifton (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>), Lasch (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1978</em>), and Kovel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1981</em>) see the search for identity as a generational issue. They argue it is caused by the collapse of old value systems. Their work has two bases: 1. A style rooted in existential literature. 2. Content based on sociology, similar to Marx's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1867</em>) idea of alienation and Durkheim's (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1951</em>) concept of anomie. Lichtenstein (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1961</em>) found a paradox in this work and in Erikson's writings. He separated ego identity (personal identity from self-objectification) from existential identity (personal identity defined by the "relationship of each soul to its mere existence"). He proposed two opposing forces: 1. The "identity principle": the drive to keep historical existence and self-created identity. 2. The longing to abandon identity, called "metamorphosis." He sees the mother-child bond as the start of identity formation. The mother imprints an "identity theme" on the infant. Sexual union shows both forces: the person becomes a tool for another's needs while also giving up their identity. The section on developmental origins starts with Freud's implicit view of identity. It includes body bases, gender differences, early identifications, the Oedipus complex, the ego's synthetic function, and ethnic and moral dimensions. Fenichel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1937</em>) expanded on this. He said the self-image comes from two sources: 1. Direct awareness of inner experience. 2. Indirect perception of the body and mind as an object. Early analysts like Federn (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1952</em>) and Glover (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1950</em>) added ideas about "ego feeling" and "ego nuclei." Spiegel (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1959</em>) described the "pooling" of isolated self-representations. Greenacre (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) proposed a continuum from body image to self-image to identity. She emphasized the habitual taking in of opposite-sex genitals and their fusion with the body image. She placed the child's early sense of identity in the phallic and oedipal phases. It is not complete until adolescence. Mahler (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1958</em>) separated the infant's psychological birth from biological birth. She listed phases: autistic, symbiotic, differentiation, practicing, rapprochement, and the road to object constancy. She set two conditions for identity formation: 1. Enteroceptive-proprioceptive stimuli must not block structure formation. This requires a shift from viscera-centered splanchnic innervation to the external rind of body ego. 2. The mother must buffer and organize stimuli for the infant. Jacobson (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1964</em>) organized development into five categories: 1. Self-and object representations. 2. Fused self-object representation as the first intrapsychic structure. 3. Identity emerging from primitive identifications. 4. The role of aggression in self-delineation. 5. Modifications of oedipal resolution and superego formation. She combined findings from treating affective disorders with the metapsychological works of Freud, Fenichel, Hartmann, Mahler, and Spitz. Winnicott's work deals with authenticity, body presence, and time continuity. He locates the true self in "the aliveness of body tissues and working of body functions." Kernberg (1967, 1976, 1980b) created a three-step process for identity formation: introjection, identification, and identity formation. He separated adolescent identity crisis from identity diffusion. He described middle age as a time of renewed recognition of personal myths and acceptance of creativity limits. Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) proposed two routes for the oedipal child: 1. The "long path" leading to ego ideal formation and temporal order. 2. The "short path" leading to perverse character organization. Blos (1962, 1965, 1967) described the second individuation process of adolescence. He identified three aspects of sexual identity consolidation. He proposed that the adult ego ideal is "the heir of the negative Oedipus complex." Stoller (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1968</em>), along with Galenson and Roiphe (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1971</em>) and Meyer (<em style="font-size:0.9em">1980</em>), contributed to gender identity consolidation. The chapter ends by listing seven characteristics of a solid identity. It defines identity diffusion as disturbance in these areas. The author argues that identity has both internal and social effects. Its origins span from early mother-infant interactions to adolescent consolidation.

## Key Distinctions

- Identity and Kernberg both crisis of adolescence versus identity diffusion: Erikson described the usual identity crisis of adolescenceThe distinction matters because identity diffusion is a structural disturbance, not a normative developmental crisis.
- Ego identity versus existential identity: Lichtenstein (1961) identified these as two views in Erikson's (1958) work. Ego identity is personal identity resulting from self-objectivation; existential identity is personal identity defined by the "relationship of each soul to its mere existence." This distinction matters because it preserves the inherent ambiguity in the concept of identity.
- The "long path" versus the "short path" in oedipal resolution: Chasseguet-Smirgel (1983, 1984) distinguished these two routes. The long path involves accepting the chronological lag between erotic longing and genital capacity, forming ego ideal and temporal order. The short path bypasses the Oedipal tragedy, fuses ego and ego ideal, and leads to perverse character organization.
- True self versus false self in Winnicott's framework: The true self emanates from the infant's spontaneous gestures and personal ideas. The false self develops when the mother fails to decode the infant's overture and replaces it with her own gesture, forcing compliance and dissociation of authentic self-assertion.
- Introjection versus identification in Kernberg's three-step process: Introjection involves internalizing affectively charged attributes without full assimilation, functioning as a "foreign body" in the self. Identification is a less concrete, more role-oriented internalization situated "deeper" in the self-system, requiring a more sophisticated ego.


## Theoretical Tensions

- Self-objectivation versus existential awareness: Lichtenstein (1961, 1963) argues that the conflict between identity as self-objectivation and identity as pure existential awareness without any external referent must be accepted as such. Erikson (1958) stated he can only approach identity "by approaching it from a variety of angles—biographic, pathographic, and theoretical; and by letting the term identity speak for itself in a number of connotations."
- The identity principle versus metamorphosis: Lichtenstein (1961) posits the perpetual drive to maintain identity against the longing to abandon the human quality of identity. He sees these as "two limits of human experience incompatible with one another but complementary," with human life existing in an oscillation between these two extremes. The loss of identity can produce confusion and anguish, but also liberation and ecstasy.
- The sociological versus the psychoanalytic account of identity: The authors Fromm (1955), Wheelis (1958), Lynd (1958), Lifton (1971), Lasch (1978), and Kovel (1981) use identity in its descriptive sense rather than its psychoanalytic sense. Their hypotheses about identity disturbance resemble Marxist depictions of alienation and Durkheimian anomie, creating a tension with the intrapsychic developmental accounts of Mahler, Jacobson, and Kernberg.
- The two-sidedness of identity itself: The chapter notes that identity is necessarily a "two-sided term with intrapsychic as well as social ramifications." This inherent ambiguity has contributed to its ambiguous place in psychoanalytic theory, with different investigators emphasizing different facets of identity and elucidating various aspects of its developmental origins.
