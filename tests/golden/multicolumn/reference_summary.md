Urban heat islands raise city temperatures several degrees above surrounding rural areas. Researchers measure the effect with dense networks of low-cost sensors across neighborhoods combined with satellite thermal imagery from cloud-free overpasses; sensors capture block-scale variation while satellites provide city-wide context. Heat island intensity is strongest on calm clear summer nights when rural landscapes cool quickly and stored urban heat is released slowly, whereas daytime differences rarely exceed two or three degrees. Wind moderates the nighttime signal by mixing warm urban air with cooler outside air. The largest recorded intensity reached nine degrees on a July night in a densely built downtown with little vegetation and extensive dark asphalt. On mitigation, green roofs cool through evapotranspiration and shading: a municipal program installed fourteen hectares between 2019 and 2023, cutting average summer afternoon roof temperatures by nearly twelve degrees. Street trees deliver comparable pedestrian-level benefits, and reflective pavement coatings increase albedo along major corridors. Cooling depends on irrigation, since unwatered street trees in semi-arid cities transpire little during heat waves, halving expected relief. Because mean radiant temperature governs pedestrian comfort, shading matters more than evaporation for sidewalks and plazas. Municipal codes increasingly mandate minimum canopy coverage for new parking lots and cool roofing, and twenty-one of thirty surveyed cities now have an urban heat ordinance, up from four a decade ago.
