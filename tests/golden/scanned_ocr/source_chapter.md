CHAPTER III . THE CANAL OF THE PHARAOHS
[Page 47]

The idea of joining the Mediterranean to the Red Sea is far older than
the steamship. Pharaoh Necho the Second is said to have begun a canal
through the Wadi Tumilat around the year 600 before Christ, and the
works were completed a century later under King Darius of Persia. This
ancient waterway, which later writers called the Canal of the Pharaohs,
allowed boats to sail from the Nile to the Bitter Lakes and thence to
the Red Sea.

[Page 48]

The old canal silted up and was restored several times. The Roman
emperor Trajan repaired it around the year 112, and the Arab conquest
of Egypt gave it a new period of usefulness. Finally, in the year 767,
the caliph al-Mansur ordered the waterway to be closed, so that
supplies could not reach rebels in Medina. For more than a thousand
years afterwards the route remained a dry ditch in the desert.

The modern history of the isthmus begins with Napoleon. In 1798 his
engineers surveyed the region and made a serious error: they judged the
level of the Red Sea to be some ten metres higher than that of the
Mediterranean, and therefore reported that a direct canal was
impossible. This mistake was corrected by a fresh survey between 1841
and 1847, which showed that the two seas differed by hardly anything at

In 1854 the French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps obtained from Said
Pasha, the viceroy of Egypt, the first concession for a maritime canal
across the isthmus. The company he founded, the Compagnie Unive rselle
du Canal Maritime de Suez, received a second and firmer concession in
1856. Work began on the 25th of April, 1859, at Port Said, a new town
named after the viceroy.

[Page 50]

The excavation was carried out chiefly by forced labour. Tens of
thousands of Egyptian peasants, the fellaheen, were rounded up under
the corvee laws and set to dig with picks and baskets. An estimated
twenty thousand labourers died during the early years of the works. The
British goverment of the day objected to the project, and Lord
Palmerston called the scheme a plot to disturb the commerce of the
world. Machines of an English inventor, the great dredgers of M. Couvreux,
were brought in after 1864, and from that time the corvee was gradually
given up.

The canal was opened to navigation on the 17th of November, 1869. It
measured 164 kilometres in length, was eight metres deep, and possessed
no locks, for the two seas stand at nearly the same level. The cost of
construction, about one hundred million francs, was more than double
the original estimate. The khedive Ismail, who had succeeded Said
Pasha, found himself so pressed for money that in 1875 he sold his
shares to the British goverment for four million pounds.

[Page 51]

The canal at once became one of the great arteries of world trade. In
its first full year some four hundred and eighty-six ships passed
through it. By the year 1900 the annual number exceeded three thousand.
The channel was deepened and widened many times, and by 1939 it could
carry ships drawing eleven metres of water. The British establishment
at Port Said, and later the garrison zone along the waterway, became a
standing source of friction with Egyptian nationahsm, until the
agreement of 1936 reduced the garrison to the neighbourhood of the
canal itself.

In July 1956 President Gamal Abdel Nasser announced the nationalization
of the Suez Canal Company, and the Egyptian state took over the
waterway. The crisis that followed brought invasion, the sinking of
some forty ships in the channel, and a long closure from 1956 to 1957.
The canal was blocked again after the war of June 1967, and it did not
reopen until the 5th of June, 1975. Since that reopening it has been
enlarged again and again, so that today it can pass the largest
merchant vessels afloat.
