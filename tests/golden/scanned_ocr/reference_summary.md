The idea of joining the Mediterranean to the Red Sea is ancient: Pharaoh Necho the Second began a canal through the Wadi Tumilat around 600 BC, completed a century later under King Darius of Persia, later restored by Trajan around 112 and closed by caliph al-Mansur in 767 to starve rebels in Medina. Napoleon's engineers wrongly reported a direct canal impossible because they judged the Red Sea ten metres higher than the Mediterranean, a mistake corrected by surveys between 1841 and 1847. Ferdinand de Lesseps obtained concessions from Said Pasha in 1854 and 1856, and work began at Port Said on 25 April 1859. The excavation relied chiefly on forced labour under the corvee laws, with an estimated twenty thousand labourers dying, until Couvreux dredgers replaced hand digging after 1864. The canal opened on 17 November 1869: 164 kilometres long, eight metres deep, lock-free, costing about one hundred million francs, and the khedive Ismail sold his shares to the British government for four million pounds in 1875. Traffic grew from under five hundred ships in the first year to more than three thousand by 1900. Nasser nationalized the canal in July 1956, and closures followed in 1956-1957 and after the 1967 war until the reopening on 5 June 1975.
