Alzheimer's disease affects approximately 55 million people worldwide according to the World Health Organization, causing progressive neurodegeneration that leads to memory loss, cognitive decline, and loss of independent living. Memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, has been approved for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's since 2003. It functions as a low-affinity, non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist, blocking the excitotoxic effects of glutamate while preserving normal physiological neurotransmission; its low affinity lets it bind during pathologically elevated glutamate levels but dissociate during normal synaptic transmission. Researchers demonstrated memantine reduces calcium influx into neurons by approximately 40%, preventing downstream tau phosphorylation and amyloid accumulation. A 24-week randomized controlled trial of 252 patients with moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's showed memantine produced a 50% improvement in cognitive scores versus placebo and meaningful functional stabilization in 65% of participants. The most common adverse effects were dizziness (7%), headache (6%), and constipation (5%), and the evidence indicates memantine represents an effective treatment option for moderate-to-severe disease, with early intervention leading to better outcomes.
