# Source Chapter: The Memantine AD Study (academic, fictionalized)

## Introduction

Alzheimer's disease affects approximately 55 million people worldwide
according to the World Health Organization. The progressive
neurodegeneration leads to memory loss, cognitive decline, and
ultimately the loss of independent living. Memantine, an NMDA receptor
antagonist, has been approved for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's since
2003.

## Mechanism of Action

Memantine functions as a low-affinity, non-competitive NMDA receptor
antagonist. The drug blocks the excitotoxic effects of glutamate while
preserving normal physiological neurotransmission. This selective
blockade is achieved through the drug's low affinity, which allows it
to bind during pathologically elevated glutamate levels but dissociate
during normal synaptic transmission.

Researchers demonstrated that memantine reduces calcium influx into
neurons by approximately 40%. The study found that this reduction
prevents the downstream cascade of tau phosphorylation and amyloid
accumulation.

## Clinical Efficacy

A 24-week randomized controlled trial enrolled 252 patients with
moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease. The results demonstrated that
memantine treatment led to a 50% improvement in cognitive scores
compared to placebo. The study indicates that the treatment causes
meaningful functional stabilization in 65% of participants.

## Safety Profile

The most common adverse effects were dizziness (7%), headache (6%),
and constipation (5%). The results show that memantine demonstrates a
favorable safety profile compared to other NMDA antagonists.

## Conclusion

Therefore memantine represents an effective treatment option for
moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease. The evidence indicates that
early intervention leads to better outcomes for patients.
