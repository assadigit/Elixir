## Thorough Summary

Chapter 9 provides a comprehensive examination of histrionic and schizotypal personality disorders, which were newly classified in DSM-III (*1980*) and represent significant nosological developments. For histrionic disorder, the chapter traces its evolution from the historical hysterical personality, framing it as a more regressive variant. Psychoanalytic literature reveals a tension between phallic-oedipal and pregenital determinants, with Easser and Lesser (*1965*) distinguishing a true hysteric from a more disturbed hysteroid variant characterized by greater ego weakness. While DSM-III-R (*1987*) revised the criteria to include sexual seductiveness, it still inadequately differentiated between healthier and more pathological variants. Ultimately, histrionic disorder is distinct from both hysterical personality and conditions like borderline and antisocial personality, sharing features with the latter but lacking their sustained ruthlessness (Kernberg *1989*, Lilienfeld et al. *1986*). The psychoanalytic foundations of histrionic personality are deeply rooted in a historical debate. Early theorists like Reich (*1933*) and Fenichel (*1945*) emphasized oedipal conflicts, associating the disorder with subtle sexual behavior, coquettishness, and suggestibility. In contrast, Freud’s (*1931*) concept of the erotic type, alongside the work of Wittels (*1930*), Marmor (*1953*), and Halleck (*1967*), highlighted pregenital fixations, portraying the hysteric's sexuality as a sham for deeper oral needs. This tension was further clarified by Easser and Lesser (*1965*), who differentiated the true hysteric—characterized by labile emotionality, active engagement with the world, and a tendency to sexually embroider relationships—from the more disturbed hysteroid. The latter, originating from broken families with inadequate maternal care, exhibits greater impulsivity, regression, and a breach of social norms. 

Kernberg (*1967*) later distinguished an infantile personality from a hysterical one, noting differences in the context of emotional lability, the nature of exhibitionism, and sexual life. 

Zetzel (*1968*) elaborated on this continuum, dividing hysterical women into four groups of increasing severity, with the most severe (good hysterics) showing significant developmental failure in basic ego functions. Subsequent contributions from Abse (*1974*), Blacker and Tupin (*1977*), and Liebowitz and Klein (*1981*) further solidified the understanding of a sicker, more regressive variant of hysterical personality, characterized by ego weakness, poor reality testing, and vulnerability to psychosis. The nosological journey of histrionic personality disorder in the DSM reflects ongoing attempts to capture its complexity. DSM-III (*1980*) introduced the term, aiming to avoid the historical baggage of hysteria, but its criteria were criticized for representing only the more regressive pole of the hysterical-infantile continuum and for omitting key features like sexual seductiveness. DSM-III-R (*1987*) attempted a revision by adding sexual seductiveness and altering the description, yet it still failed to adequately differentiate between healthier and more pathological forms. The chapter argues that histrionic personality disorder, as currently defined, corresponds to what was previously termed hysteroid" or "infantile personality, characterized by identity diffusion, generalized emotional lability, primitive defense mechanisms, and an underlying borderline personality organization. It is distinct from the traditional hysterical personality, which warrants separate inclusion in future diagnostic manuals. Differential diagnoses are crucial, as histrionic disorder shares features with borderline and antisocial personality disorders but differs in its surface-level charm, suggestibility, and lack of the sustained ruthlessness seen in antisocial individuals. Schizotypal personality disorder, also introduced in DSM-III, emerged from two distinct traditions: observations of eccentric relatives of schizophrenics and the study of nonpsychotic individuals with schizophrenia-like symptoms. The first tradition stems from Bleuler (*1908*, *1911*), who coined schizoid personality to describe a morbidly exaggerated inner life at the expense of external reality, noting these traits among relatives of schizophrenics. This concept was diluted by psychoanalytic interpretations, which expanded the term to include less severe pathologies and normal infantile positions, necessitating a redefinition of the schizophrenia-spectrum personality. The second tradition originated with Bleuler’s (*1911*) description of latent schizophrenia, a form containing all core symptoms of schizophrenia in attenuated expression. This was elaborated by Zilboorg (*1941*) with ambulatory schizophrenia and by Hoch and Polatin (*1949*) with pseudoneurotic schizophrenia, characterized by multiple neurotic symptoms, anxiety, and polymorphous perverse sexuality. Theoretical underpinnings were provided by Rado (*1953*), who hypothesized a constitutional defect (schizotaxia) involving deficits in integrating pleasurable experiences and distorted bodily awareness, and by Meehl (*1962*), who emphasized an integrative neural deficit. Genetic studies by Kety et al. (*1968*, *1975*) further solidified the concept, identifying borderline schizophrenia among schizophrenic relatives, characterized by odd thinking, brief cognitive disorganization, and chronic anhedonia. (*1979*), focused on cognitive-perceptual disturbances such as magical thinking, ideas of reference, and odd speech. However, subsequent research by Gunderson and Siever (*1983*), Kendler et al. (*1981*), and McGlashan (*1987*) found that social isolation, odd communication, and suspiciousness were more discriminative of the disorder. It also distinguishes schizotypal from borderline personality disorder, noting that while both may exist on a spectrum, schizotypal is more strongly linked to schizophrenia genetics and characterized by oddness and social withdrawal rather than the identity diffusion and affective instability of borderline disorder.
