## Executive Summary

This chapter examines histrionic and schizotypal personality disorders, tracing their relatively recent introduction in DSM-III (*1980*) and their conceptual evolution from psychoanalytic and psychiatric traditions. For histrionic disorder, the chapter highlights conflicting psychoanalytic views on its origins, critiques DSM-III and DSM-III-R criteria for failing to capture its full spectrum, and concludes it represents a regressive borderline organization distinct from healthier hysterical personalities. Schizotypal disorder is shown to have evolved from studies of schizoid relatives of schizophrenics and "latent schizophrenia," with genetic studies supporting its theoretical underpinnings; the author argues DSM-III-R’s shift from cognitive to interpersonal criteria was necessary but notes a conceptual inconsistency in excluding affective-spectrum disorders from the personality disorder category. The chapter generalizes to all severe personality disorders, emphasizing their fluid nature due to frequent overlaps (e.g., narcissistic-paranoid), variable social functioning, sex-related differences in incidence (e.g., narcissistic/antisocial more common in men, histrionic/borderline in women), lifespan changes (e.g., narcissistic patients stabilizing in middle age), and cultural influences (e.g., grandiose asceticism vs. materialism in narcissism). Ultimately, the chapter posits that severe personality disorders are not discrete entities but are shaped by multiple interacting factors, urging further research into their developmental and transcultural variations.
