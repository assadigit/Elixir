## Names
- **Tausk** (1919) — introduced the term identity into psychoanalytic literature, examining how the child discovers their self.
- **Freud** (1921, 1923a, 1923b, 1924, 1925b, 1926a, 1926b) — rarely used the term identity technically but provided implicit views on identity’s genesis, including somatic underpinnings, gender differences, and early identifications.
- **Erikson** (1950a, 1956, 1958, 1959) — resurrected the term identity in character formation, defining ego identity as persistent sameness within oneself and sharing essential character with others.
- **Jacobson** (1953a, 1954a, 1954b, 1957, 1959, 1964) — contributed a developmental framework for identity, emphasizing self-and object representations, fused self-object representations, and the role of aggression in self-delineation.
- **Mahler** (1958a, 1958b, 1968, 1975) — distinguished the psychological birth of the infant, outlining phases of separation-individuation leading to self-constancy and gender identity.
- **Kernberg** (1967, 1976, 1978, 1980b) — emphasized the psychostructural significance of identity diffusion in borderline personality organization, proposing a three-step process of identity formation.
- **Lichtenstein** (1961, 1963) — highlighted the tension between identity as self-objectivation and existential awareness, proposing the identity principle and metamorphosis as opposing forces.
- **Fenichel** (1937, 1945) — suggested the self-image originates from inner experience and bodily perception, emphasizing the body image’s importance for ego development.
- **Federn** (1952) — conceptualized early “ego feeling” as rudimentary self-representations forming the building blocks of identity.
- **Glover** (1950) — introduced the concept of “ego nuclei,” islets of executive function that fuse to form the ego.
- **Spiegel** (1959) — discussed the “pooling” of isolated self-representations as underlying solid identity formation.
- **Greenacre** (1958a, 1958b) — emphasized the importance of opposite-sex genital perception and bisexuality in identity formation, linking it to body image and self-image.
- **Winnicott** (1949, 1960) — provided the developmental basis for authenticity, corporeality, and temporal continuity in the self, distinguishing between true and false selves.
- **Chasseguet-Smirgel** (1983, 1984) — explored ego-ideal formation and perverse character organization, emphasizing the oedipal phase’s role in identity consolidation.
- **Blos** (1962, 1965, 1967, 1972, 1974, 1984) — outlined intrapsychic processes of adolescence, including the second individuation process and consolidation of sexual identity.
- **Fromm** (1955) — contributed to sociocultural determinants of personal identity.
- **Wheelis** (1958) — emphasized the social aspect of identity.
- **Lynd** (1958) — explored identity as a general problem for the present generation.
- **Lifton** (1971) — examined identity in the context of social breakdown.
- **Lasch** (1978) — analyzed identity disturbance in modern society.
- **Kovel** (1981) — contributed to sociological perspectives on identity.
- **Searles** (1960) — discussed the longing to abandon human quality of identity.
- **Stoller** (1968, 1972) — contributed to gender identity consolidation.
- **Galenson and Roiphe** (1971) — explored gender identity development.
- **Meyer** (1980) — contributed to gender identity research.
- **Hartmann** (1948, 1950, 1952, 1955) — differentiated ego and self, influencing Erikson’s later work.
- **Kris** (1946) — collaborated with Hartmann and Loewenstein on ego psychology.
- **Loewenstein** (1946) — contributed to ego psychology and superego formation.
- **Loewald** (1951) — highlighted the father’s role in psychic structuring.
- **Klein** (1948) — influenced Kernberg’s integration of object relations theory.

## Dates
- **1919** — Tausk introduced the term identity into psychoanalytic literature.
- **1920s** — Freud rarely used the term identity technically, with only one technical mention in 1926a.
- **1950s** — Erikson resurrected the term identity in character formation; Jacobson and Mahler contributed to object relations and developmental models.
- **1960s** — Kernberg emphasized identity diffusion in borderline personality organization; Blos and Eissler contributed to adolescent identity development.
- **1970s** — Mahler and colleagues published on separation-individuation; Chasseguet-Smirgel explored ego-ideal formation.
- **1980s** — Kernberg consolidated views on identity diffusion and middle-age identity development; Blos expanded on adolescent processes.

## Terms
- **identity** — a multifaceted concept involving persistent sameness within oneself, sharing essential character with others, and a sense of individual continuity.
- **ego identity** — Erikson’s term for both selfsameness and sharing essential character with others, later dropping the “ego” prefix.
- **identity diffusion** — a syndrome characterized by poorly synthesized identities and contradictions in self-image, central to borderline and narcissistic disorders.
- **self-constancy** — Mahler’s term for a stable sense of one’s unique and separate personhood, emerging at the end of the rapprochement subphase.
- **identity theme** — Lichtenstein’s concept of an irreversible pattern imprinted by the mother, capable of variations influencing creativity or neurosis.
- **metamorphosis** — Lichtenstein’s term for the longing to abandon human quality of identity, experienced as liberation or ecstasy.
- **false self** — Winnicott’s concept of an inauthentic persona developed when the mother fails to validate the infant’s true self.
- **true self** — Winnicott’s concept of the authentic self emerging from spontaneous gestures and personal ideas when the mother is “good enough.”
- **second individuation** — Blos’s term for the adolescent process of emotional disengagement from internalized infantile objects, leading to greater autonomy.
- **ego ideal** — Chasseguet-Smirgel’s and Blos’s concept of a cherished personality attribute originating from father attachment and the negative Oedipus complex.
- **anomie** — Durkheim’s concept of individual alienation due to rapid social change and fragmentation.
- **going-on-being** — Winnicott’s term for the infant’s natural tendency to become an integrated unit with temporal continuity.
- **object constancy** — Mahler’s term for the internalization of a sustained, ambivalent object representation during separation-individuation.
- **bisexual identifications** — Greenacre’s and Blos’s concept of early identifications with both sexes, influencing later gender identity.
- **identity crisis** — Blos’s and Kernberg’s term for the adolescent turmoil in consolidating identity, distinct from identity diffusion.
- **perverse character** — Chasseguet-Smirgel’s term for a character organization bypassing the oedipal complex through a “short path.”
