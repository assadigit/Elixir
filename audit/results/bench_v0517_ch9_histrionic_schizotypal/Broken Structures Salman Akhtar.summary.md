The psychoanalytic elaboration of the histrionic entity was significantly advanced by Zetzel (1968), who divided hysterical women into four groups of increasing psychopathological severity to map the dynamics and phenomenology of the continuum. Building on this, Abse (1974) distinguished "hysteriform borderline personalities" from the hysterical personality proper, noting their pronounced oral traits and greater vulnerability to severe regression, while Blacker and Tupin (1977) described a more pregenitally fixated or narcissistically vulnerable hysteric characterized by ego weakness and poor differentiation of reality. Cooper’s (1987) critique of DSM-III further highlighted the omission of sexual seductiveness and the failure to distinguish between healthier hysterical personalities and more infantile ones, arguing for two separate diagnostic entities based on Tupin’s synthesis of the literature. Finally, Gunderson (1985a) clarified the differential diagnosis between borderline and histrionic disorders by noting that while both are manipulative and dependent, the borderline individual is more chronically enraged and vituperative, whereas the histrionic individual remains less overtly self-destructive and easier to get along with on the surface.
