## Key Distinctions

- Paranoid personality disorder vs. paranoid schizophrenia: The chapter distinguishes these by noting that paranoid schizophrenia involves a "serious break with reality," bizarre delusions, auditory hallucinations, and thought control symptoms, which are absent in paranoid personality disorder (p. 156).
- Paranoid personality disorder vs. phobic character: Distinction is drawn on the basis that phobic characters avoid situations (not people), lack the personalization/intentionality of paranoid paranoia, and react with anxiety/flight rather than rage/attack (p. 157).
- Paranoid personality disorder vs. compulsive personality disorder: Differentiated by the source of moral pressure (external/unjustified for paranoid vs. internalized/superior for compulsive), cognitive style (narrowly focused/biased vs. overinclusive), and level of character organization (lower/borderline with identity diffusion vs. higher/neurotic with consolidated identity) (p. 158).
- Paranoid personality disorder vs. narcissistic personality disorder: Distinguished by the paranoid's pervasive mistrust, litigiousness, and lack of seductiveness/charming social veneer, contrasted with the narcissist's inordinate exploitativeness, charming socialization, and absentminded disregard for details (p. 159).
- Paranoid personality disorder vs. antisocial personality disorder: Differentiated by the lack of "chronicity of antisocial behavior" and the absence of a child-to-adult continuum in paranoid personalities; their acts are driven by projection/paranoia rather than the stimulating quality typical of psychopaths (p. 159).

- Descriptive psychiatry vs. psychoanalytic literature: The chapter opens by noting that unlike other severe personality disorders, these two views of paranoid personality disorder "continue to exist independently of each other," and the author's aim is to synthesize them (p. 138).
- Freudian formulation vs. critiques regarding aggression and environment: A tension exists between Freud's view that paranoia stems from the repudiation of latent homosexuality through projection, which has been criticized for "overemphasizing libidinal factors," ignoring actual harshness faced by the individual, and minimizing the role of aggression (p. 142).
- Libidinal defense vs. primary hostility: Blum (1980) argues that while Freud's view treats paranoia as a defense against homosexuality, "hostility is a primary problem in this condition and not merely a defense," suggesting a debate over whether aggression or libidinal conflict is the core driver (p. 146).
